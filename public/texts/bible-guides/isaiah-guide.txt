ABOUT ISAIAH

Isaiah is one of the grandest books in Scripture. It moves from Judah’s sin and coming judgment to breathtaking promises of comfort, restoration, a righteous king, a suffering servant, the gathering of the nations, and finally new heavens and a new earth.

The historical Isaiah ministered in Judah during the eighth century BC, through the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. Assyria was the great imperial threat. The book also looks beyond Isaiah’s immediate generation to Babylonian exile and restoration.

Christians have always read Isaiah as one of the Old Testament books that most clearly prepares for Christ. The New Testament quotes Isaiah repeatedly when explaining Jesus’ birth, ministry, rejection, death, resurrection hope, mission to the nations, and final kingdom.

A useful large-scale outline is:

Isaiah 1–39 — Judah’s sin, Assyria, judgment, and the promised king
Isaiah 40–55 — comfort, return, the servant, and God’s saving power
Isaiah 56–66 — restored people, true worship, justice, and new creation

1. ISAIAH 1 — GOD’S CASE AGAINST HIS PEOPLE

Isaiah begins like a courtroom.

God has raised children who have rebelled. Judah continues religious activity—sacrifices, festivals, prayers—but social injustice makes the worship offensive.

This is one of Isaiah’s central themes: worship and righteousness cannot be separated.

God says, in effect:
Stop doing evil.
Learn to do good.
Seek justice.
Correct oppression.
Defend the fatherless.
Plead for the widow.

Then comes grace:
“Though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow.”

Christian faith does not teach that ritual can compensate for injustice. God desires hearts and lives turned toward him.

2. ISAIAH 2–5 — THE LORD’S VINEYARD AND THE FAILURE OF JUDAH

Isaiah sees both judgment and future hope.

In chapter 2, nations stream to the mountain of the LORD to learn his ways. Swords become plowshares.

This is the opposite of Babel: the nations are not gathering to make themselves great but to receive God’s instruction.

Yet present Judah is proud, wealthy, unjust, and idolatrous.

Isaiah 5 gives the song of the vineyard. God carefully prepares a vineyard but receives bad fruit.

The Hebrew wordplay is powerful:
God looked for justice but found bloodshed.
He looked for righteousness but heard cries of distress.

Privilege creates responsibility.

Israel was chosen to bear good fruit.

CHRISTIAN CONNECTION

Jesus later uses vineyard imagery when confronting Israel’s leaders.

The principle remains:
God’s gifts are meant to produce faithful fruit.

3. ISAIAH 6 — “HOLY, HOLY, HOLY”

Isaiah sees the LORD enthroned in the temple.

Seraphim cry:
“Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts.”

Holiness means more than moral purity. God is utterly distinct, majestic, glorious, and worthy.

Isaiah’s first response is not confidence.

“Woe is me.”

He recognizes his own uncleanness.

A coal from the altar touches his lips, and his guilt is removed.

Notice the order:

Isaiah sees God.
Isaiah sees himself truthfully.
God provides cleansing.
Then Isaiah is commissioned.

“Here am I. Send me.”

Christian service begins with grace, not self-importance.

THE HARDENING MESSAGE

Isaiah is told that his ministry will often meet resistance. Jesus later quotes this chapter to explain hardened responses to his own teaching.

The Word can soften the humble and expose the hardness of those determined not to hear.

4. ISAIAH 7–12 — IMMANUEL AND THE PROMISED KING

Judah is threatened during the reign of Ahaz.

God tells Ahaz to trust rather than build his security on political alliances.

Ahaz refuses the sign God offers.

Then comes the famous Immanuel prophecy:
“Behold, the virgin/young woman shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel.”

The immediate prophecy addresses Ahaz’s historical crisis. Matthew later sees its fullest significance in the birth of Jesus.

This teaches an important principle of biblical prophecy: a passage can have a real historical meaning in its own setting while also participating in a larger pattern that reaches fulfillment in Christ.

ISAIAH 9

A child is promised:
Wonderful Counselor,
Mighty God,
Everlasting Father,
Prince of Peace.

His government will rest on David’s throne.

ISAIAH 11

A shoot comes from Jesse—the father of David.

The Spirit rests upon him.
He judges with righteousness.
The wolf and lamb imagery pictures a restored creation under righteous rule.

CHRISTIAN CONNECTION

Christians confess Jesus as the Son of David and the promised ruler whose kingdom ultimately brings justice and peace.

5. ISAIAH 13–23 — ORACLES AGAINST THE NATIONS

Isaiah turns outward to Babylon, Assyria, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, Egypt, Tyre, and others.

The message is important:
God is not merely Judah’s tribal deity.

He is Lord of nations.

Empires imagine themselves permanent.

Isaiah says they are not.

Pride, violence, exploitation, and military power do not place a nation outside God’s judgment.

BABYLON

Babylon becomes more than a geographical empire in biblical imagination. It becomes an image of proud human power organized against God.

Revelation later develops that symbolism.

ASSYRIA

Assyria is used as an instrument of judgment, yet Assyria itself is judged for arrogance.

That teaches an important doctrine of providence:
God may use human powers without approving their motives.

6. ISAIAH 24–27 — THE LORD’S WORLDWIDE VICTORY

These chapters widen the horizon dramatically.

Judgment becomes worldwide.

Death itself becomes an enemy God will defeat.

Isaiah 25 contains an extraordinary promise:
God will prepare a feast for all peoples.
He will swallow up death forever.
He will wipe away tears.

The New Testament later uses this language in its resurrection hope.

Isaiah does not merely promise that Judah will survive Assyria.

The ultimate hope is much larger:
God will defeat death.

CHRISTIAN CONNECTION

Paul echoes Isaiah when celebrating resurrection:
“Death is swallowed up in victory.”

Revelation echoes the promise that God will wipe away every tear.

7. ISAIAH 28–35 — FALSE SECURITY AND THE HIGHWAY OF HOLINESS

Judah repeatedly seeks security through political schemes.

Isaiah exposes the temptation to trust:
Egypt,
alliances,
horses,
military planning,
rather than God.

This does not mean planning is sinful.

It means no strategy can replace faithfulness to God.

Isaiah 30 describes people who want prophets to tell them pleasant things.

“Speak to us smooth things.”

That temptation has never disappeared.

Faithful preaching cannot simply confirm what listeners already want.

ISAIAH 35

The section ends in joy:
desert blooming,
weak hands strengthened,
blind seeing,
deaf hearing,
a highway of holiness,
the redeemed returning with singing.

Judgment is not Isaiah’s final word.

8. ISAIAH 36–39 — HEZEKIAH, ASSYRIA, AND THE LIMITS OF HUMAN KINGS

These chapters tell the historical story behind much of Isaiah’s preaching.

Sennacherib’s Assyrian army threatens Jerusalem.

The Assyrian spokesman mocks Hezekiah’s trust in God.

Hezekiah brings the crisis before the LORD.

Jerusalem is delivered.

This is one of Hezekiah’s finest moments.

But the story does not end there.

Hezekiah later proudly displays his treasures to Babylonian envoys.

Isaiah announces that Babylon will eventually carry these treasures away.

Even a relatively faithful king is not the final king.

Human leaders can trust God magnificently in one chapter and act foolishly in another.

9. ISAIAH 40 — “COMFORT, COMFORT MY PEOPLE”

The tone changes.

The horizon now includes exile.

The message is comfort.

A voice cries:
“Prepare the way of the LORD.”

The New Testament applies this language to John the Baptist preparing for Jesus.

Isaiah contrasts God with idols.

Nations are like a drop in a bucket before him.

Yet this enormous God cares for weak people.

“He gives power to the faint.”

Those who wait for the LORD renew their strength.

THE CHRISTIAN BALANCE

Biblical faith holds together:
God’s greatness,
and God’s tenderness.

A God small enough to be managed would not be worth trusting.

The God who rules the stars also strengthens the exhausted.

10. ISAIAH 41–48 — GOD, IDOLS, CYRUS, AND THE LORD OF HISTORY

God challenges idols to explain history.

They cannot.

The LORD declares that history is under his rule.

CYRUS

One of Isaiah’s most striking features is the naming of Cyrus, the Persian ruler who will permit exiles to return.

Isaiah even calls Cyrus God’s “anointed” in a specific political sense.

Cyrus is not presented as Israel’s Messiah in the later Christian sense.

He is a foreign ruler used by God.

Again we see providence:
God can accomplish his purposes through people who do not fully know him.

IDOLATRY

Isaiah mocks the absurdity of making a god from wood:
part is burned for heat,
part becomes an idol,
then the worshiper bows to what his own hands made.

The deeper issue is not primitive statues.

Idolatry is trusting created things as ultimate.

Modern idols may be:
money,
nation,
career,
political power,
romance,
self,
technology,
or approval.

11. ISAIAH’S SERVANT

Isaiah speaks repeatedly about the LORD’s “servant.”

Sometimes the servant is clearly Israel.

At other times the servant appears distinct from Israel and called to restore Israel.

This creates a rich biblical pattern.

Key servant passages include:
Isaiah 42
Isaiah 49
Isaiah 50
Isaiah 52:13–53:12

The servant is:
chosen,
Spirit-empowered,
gentle,
faithful,
rejected,
suffering,
and ultimately vindicated.

The New Testament repeatedly applies servant language to Jesus.

12. ISAIAH 52:13–53:12 — THE SUFFERING SERVANT

This is one of the most important passages in Christian interpretation of the Old Testament.

The servant is despised and rejected.

He suffers.

Yet his suffering is described in relation to the sins of others:

“He was pierced for our transgressions.”

“The LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all.”

The servant appears innocent yet suffers on behalf of the guilty.

Then he is vindicated.

JEWISH AND CHRISTIAN READING

Jewish interpreters have understood the servant in several ways, often in relation to Israel or a faithful remnant.

Christians see the passage reaching its fulfillment in Jesus.

The New Testament itself makes this connection:
Philip explains Isaiah 53 in relation to Jesus in Acts 8.
Peter echoes it in 1 Peter.
Jesus uses servant language for his own mission.

ATONEMENT

Isaiah 53 contributes important biblical language for understanding Christ’s death:
substitution,
bearing sin,
innocent suffering,
and justification.

No single Old Testament image explains the cross completely, but Isaiah 53 is central.

13. ISAIAH 54–55 — EVERLASTING LOVE AND THE FREE INVITATION

After the suffering servant comes restoration.

God speaks of covenant love.

Then Isaiah 55 gives one of Scripture’s great invitations:

“Come, everyone who thirsts.”

The gift is freely offered.

“Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price.”

This is grace.

God’s thoughts are higher than ours.

His word accomplishes its purpose like rain bringing life to the earth.

The chapter ends with creation itself rejoicing.

CHRISTIAN CONNECTION

Jesus repeatedly uses thirst, living water, bread, and free invitation language.

Revelation ends with:
“Let the one who is thirsty come.”

14. ISAIAH 56–59 — TRUE WORSHIP, JUSTICE, AND THE PROBLEM OF SIN

The restored community still needs transformation.

Isaiah again connects worship with justice.

Fasting means little if people exploit workers and ignore the hungry.

Isaiah 58 calls for:
loosening oppression,
feeding the hungry,
housing the poor,
and caring for the vulnerable.

ISAIAH 59

The problem is not that God is too weak to save.

“Your iniquities have made a separation between you and your God.”

Human sin remains the obstacle.

Then God himself acts as the divine warrior to bring justice.

The Bible’s hope ultimately rests on God doing what sinful humanity cannot accomplish alone.

15. ISAIAH 60–62 — ZION, LIGHT, AND GOOD NEWS

Zion is pictured shining with God’s glory.

Nations come to the light.

Isaiah 61 contains the words Jesus reads in the synagogue at Nazareth:

“The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me,
because the LORD has anointed me to bring good news to the poor...”

Jesus says:
“Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing.”

That makes Isaiah 61 a key text for understanding Jesus’ mission.

Good news includes:
liberty,
healing,
comfort,
restoration,
and divine favor.

The Messiah’s mission is not merely private spirituality.

God’s kingdom reaches broken human life.

16. ISAIAH 63–66 — PRAYER, JUDGMENT, AND NEW CREATION

Isaiah ends with tension.

God’s people pray for him to act.

Sin remains real.

Judgment remains real.

But so does breathtaking hope.

Isaiah 65 promises:
“new heavens and a new earth.”

Revelation later takes up the same language.

The Bible does not end with souls escaping creation.

It ends with creation renewed.

Isaiah’s final vision includes both universal worship and divine judgment.

Grace does not mean evil becomes unimportant.

God’s final kingdom brings both restoration and justice.

17. MAJOR THEMES IN ISAIAH

THE HOLINESS OF GOD
Isaiah never lets us reduce God to a spiritual helper. God is the Holy One of Israel.

TRUST
Judah repeatedly faces the choice between trusting God and trusting human power as ultimate.

JUSTICE
True worship produces righteousness and concern for the vulnerable.

THE KING
Isaiah expects a righteous Davidic ruler.

THE SERVANT
God’s saving purpose advances through the servant’s obedience and suffering.

THE NATIONS
Israel’s God intends salvation to reach beyond Israel.

COMFORT
Judgment is severe but not God’s final purpose for his covenant people.

NEW CREATION
Isaiah’s hope ultimately reaches the renewal of the world.

CHRIST
The New Testament finds Isaiah’s king, servant, good news, light, highway, and new creation themes converging in Jesus.

19. READING ISAIAH AS A CHRISTIAN WITHOUT FLATTENING IT

Isaiah speaks first into real historical situations.

Ahaz really faced political threat.
Hezekiah really faced Assyria.
Judah really faced judgment.
Exiles really needed comfort.

Christian reading should begin there.

Then we follow the way the New Testament itself reads Isaiah.

Matthew sees Immanuel language fulfilled in Jesus.
John the Baptist fulfills the voice crying in the wilderness.
Jesus reads Isaiah 61 as describing his mission.
The apostles use Isaiah 53 to explain Christ’s suffering.
Paul uses Isaiah when discussing Israel and the nations.
Revelation draws heavily on Isaiah’s new-creation hope.

This gives Christians a disciplined pattern.

Do not begin by forcing every verse directly into a prediction about Jesus.

First ask:
What did Isaiah mean in context?

Then ask:
How does the rest of Scripture develop this theme?

Finally ask:
How does it reach its fullness in Christ?

That approach makes Christ-centered reading stronger, not weaker.

20. DIFFICULT ISSUES IN ISAIAH

THE VIRGIN IN ISAIAH 7:14

The Hebrew word can refer to a young woman of marriageable age. Matthew’s Greek quotation uses a word meaning virgin.

Christians should not pretend the historical context disappears.

The sign speaks into Ahaz’s crisis.
Matthew then sees a greater fulfillment in Jesus’ virginal conception.

This is a good example of biblical fulfillment being larger than simple one-to-one prediction.

THE “LUCIFER” PASSAGE

Isaiah 14 describes the fall of the king of Babylon using language about the “morning star.”

Later Christian tradition connected the passage with Satan.

That association may be theologically suggestive because pride lies behind both images, but Isaiah’s immediate target is a proud human ruler.

THE SUFFERING SERVANT

Christians rightly see Christ in Isaiah 53 because the New Testament itself does.

But we should still understand how the servant theme develops across Isaiah before jumping immediately to the cross.

DIVINE JUDGMENT

Isaiah contains severe judgment.

Christian readers should neither erase it nor enjoy it.

God’s judgment reveals that evil matters and that oppression will not continue forever.

21. ISAIAH AND THE CHARACTER OF GOD

Isaiah gives one of Scripture’s fullest portraits of God.

GOD IS HOLY

He is not simply “the man upstairs.”

Isaiah trembles before the Holy One.

GOD IS SOVEREIGN

Empires appear enormous to humans.

Before God, nations are like dust on scales.

GOD IS JUST

He hears the cries produced by oppression.

Religious activity cannot bribe him.

GOD IS MERCIFUL

“Comfort, comfort my people.”

Judgment does not cancel covenant compassion.

GOD IS CREATOR

Because God made the world, he can restore it.

New creation is possible because creation was his work in the first place.

GOD SAVES

Isaiah repeatedly insists that salvation belongs to the LORD.

Idols cannot save.
Empires cannot save.
Human wisdom cannot save.

This makes Isaiah profoundly Christ-centered for Christians: the New Testament identifies Jesus with the saving work and divine glory Isaiah attributes to the LORD.
