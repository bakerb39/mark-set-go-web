1 SAMUEL
Mark, Set, Go! Bible Guide

ABOUT THIS BOOK

1 Samuel tells the transition from judges to monarchy through Samuel, Saul, and David. It explores prayer, prophetic authority, kingship, obedience, leadership, jealousy, friendship, and the question of what kind of king God’s people need.

BOOK STRUCTURE

1 Samuel 1–7 — Samuel
1 Samuel 8–15 — Saul rises and falls
1 Samuel 16–20 — David anointed, Goliath, Jonathan
1 Samuel 21–31 — David pursued, Saul declines

HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE

Read the biblical text first whenever possible. Then use the guide to trace the argument, narrative movement, theology, historical context, difficult passages, and connections to the rest of Scripture. The goal is serious understanding without turning the guide into a technical commentary.

HISTORICAL, LITERARY, AND READING CONTEXT

HISTORICAL AND LITERARY CONTEXT

1 Samuel stands at a major constitutional transition in Israel’s history. Samuel belongs to the world of judges but also functions as prophet, priestly figure, and kingmaker. Saul inaugurates monarchy; David is anointed before Saul’s reign is finished.

The book therefore asks what legitimate authority looks like. Israel wants visible political security, but the narrative repeatedly insists that even kings remain under God’s word.

The contrast between Saul and David should not be reduced to “bad man versus good man.” Saul is tragic, and David is still developing. The deeper distinction concerns response to God, repentance, patience, and the use of power.

THEOLOGY OF KINGSHIP

The monarchy is both permitted and dangerous. Deuteronomy had already allowed a king while restricting royal accumulation. 1 Samuel shows why those safeguards matter.

The king cannot replace God as Israel’s true ruler. The best king must himself be obedient.

This becomes the foundation for messianic hope: Israel does not merely need a powerful king; it needs a king whose heart is rightly ordered under God.

READING THIS BOOK WITH THE REST OF SCRIPTURE

A strong reading of 1 Samuel moves in three directions.

FIRST: READ BACKWARD.

Ask what earlier Scripture this book assumes. Covenant promises, creation, Exodus, law, priesthood, kingship, wisdom, and earlier family history often give later passages their meaning.

SECOND: READ FORWARD.

Ask what problems remain unresolved. Does the book achieve final rest? Does the king remain faithful? Does sacrifice permanently remove sin? Does wisdom conquer death? Does reform change the heart? The unresolved questions become part of the Bible’s forward movement.

THIRD: READ CANONICALLY.

Follow how later biblical writers actually use the book. This is more reliable than inventing symbolic connections. When the New Testament quotes, echoes, or develops a passage, pay attention to both continuity and transformation.

This three-direction method helps Christian readers avoid two mistakes: treating the Old Testament as if Christ were irrelevant, or treating every Old Testament detail as a secret code whose only purpose was to predict Jesus.

HOW THIS BOOK POINTS FORWARD TO CHRIST

The book develops the category of the LORD’s “anointed,” which becomes foundational for messianic hope.

David is a better king than Saul but still not the final king.

Jesus is the true anointed king who refuses illegitimate seizure of power and rules through faithful obedience.

DIFFICULT PASSAGES AND READING CAUTIONS

THE AMALEK COMMAND — A difficult judgment text requiring covenant and ancient-war context.
SAUL’S TROUBLING SPIRIT — Theological language about divine judgment and psychological decline should be handled carefully.
DAVID’S VIOLENCE — David is not morally ideal simply because he is chosen.
ENDORSING MONARCHY — The book is more nuanced than “kings are bad” or “kings are good.”
