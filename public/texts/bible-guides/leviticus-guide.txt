LEVITICUS
Mark, Set, Go! Bible Guide

ABOUT THIS BOOK

Leviticus explains how a holy God can dwell among a sinful people. It focuses on sacrifice, priesthood, purity, atonement, holiness, worship, and life ordered around God’s presence.

The book can feel distant because its rituals belong to ancient Israel’s covenant life. But its central question is deeply Christian: How can sinful people live near the holy God who has redeemed them?

BOOK STRUCTURE

Leviticus 1–7 — offerings
Leviticus 8–10 — priests ordained and judged
Leviticus 11–15 — clean and unclean
Leviticus 16 — Day of Atonement
Leviticus 17–22 — holiness in ordinary life
Leviticus 23–25 — feasts, Sabbath, Jubilee
Leviticus 26–27 — covenant blessing, curse, vows

HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE

Read the biblical text first whenever possible. Then use the guide to trace the argument, narrative movement, theology, historical context, difficult passages, and connections to the rest of Scripture. The goal is serious understanding without turning the guide into a technical commentary.

1. LEVITICUS 1–7 — SACRIFICE AND APPROACH

The offerings include burnt, grain, peace, sin/purification, and guilt/reparation offerings. They are not all “payment for sin” in the same way. Some express worship, thanksgiving, fellowship, cleansing, or restitution.

Sacrifice teaches that approach to God is costly and ordered. Blood symbolizes life given before God. The worshiper does not stroll casually into holiness on self-defined terms.

2. LEVITICUS 8–10 — PRIESTS AND HOLY SERVICE

Aaron and his sons are ordained for priestly service.

Then Nadab and Abihu offer unauthorized fire and die.

The story is severe but establishes a central principle: those who minister near God must treat his holiness seriously.

Spiritual leadership is privilege joined to responsibility.

3. LEVITICUS 11–15 — CLEAN AND UNCLEAN

Clean/unclean categories concern ritual status, not simply moral guilt.

Food, childbirth, skin conditions, and bodily discharges can render someone temporarily unclean without implying personal sin.

The system teaches Israel to distinguish holy/common and clean/unclean throughout ordinary life.

Jesus later transforms how purity is understood, emphasizing defilement from the heart and bringing cleansing to the unclean.

4. LEVITICUS 16 — THE DAY OF ATONEMENT

The Day of Atonement is the theological center of Leviticus.

The high priest enters the Most Holy Place with blood.

One goat is sacrificed.
Another—the scapegoat—symbolically carries Israel’s sins away.

Atonement involves both cleansing from defilement and removal of guilt.

Hebrews later uses this chapter extensively to explain Christ’s priestly work.

5. LEVITICUS 17–20 — “BE HOLY, FOR I AM HOLY”

Holiness is not limited to temple ritual.

These chapters address sexuality, honesty, family, justice, treatment of foreigners, care for the poor, and love of neighbor.

Leviticus 19:18 commands:
“Love your neighbor as yourself.”

Jesus later identifies this as one of the greatest commandments.

Holiness is God-shaped life.

6. LEVITICUS 21–22 — PRIESTLY HOLINESS

Priests have heightened requirements because they represent the people before God.

Some regulations reflect Israel’s ritual-symbolic world and should not be transferred directly into modern judgments about human worth.

The principle is representative responsibility, not that people with physical disabilities possess less dignity.

7. LEVITICUS 23 — SACRED TIME

Israel’s calendar includes Sabbath, Passover, Weeks, Trumpets, Day of Atonement, and Booths.

Time itself becomes part of worship.

The feasts teach people to remember:
creation,
deliverance,
harvest,
atonement,
and wilderness dependence.

Biblical faith is sustained through rhythms, not only ideas.

8. LEVITICUS 25 — SABBATH YEAR AND JUBILEE

The land rests every seventh year.

Jubilee includes return of ancestral property and release from forms of debt-servitude.

The system places limits on permanent accumulation and reminds Israel:
“The land is mine.”

Human ownership is therefore stewardship under God.

The Jubilee ideal becomes important background for Jesus’ proclamation of good news and release.

Blessing and curse reflect Israel’s covenant relationship.

Obedience brings life in the land.

Judgment does not erase God’s faithfulness.

HISTORICAL, LITERARY, AND READING CONTEXT

HISTORICAL AND LITERARY CONTEXT

Leviticus takes place almost entirely at Sinai. Unlike Exodus or Numbers, there is very little travel. Israel has been redeemed and the tabernacle has been erected, but Exodus ended with Moses unable to enter because the glory filled it. Leviticus answers the next question: how can sinful and impure people live with a holy God in their midst?

The book assumes an ancient symbolic world in which worship, body, food, time, land, sex, disease, priesthood, and sacrifice are interconnected. Modern readers often divide life into “religious” and “ordinary” categories; Leviticus resists that division.

The repeated command “be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy” gives the book its moral center. Holiness includes worship but also honesty, justice, sexuality, family responsibility, generosity, and love of neighbor.

READING LEVITICUS WELL

Do not read every regulation as though it carries the same function. Ask whether a passage concerns sacrifice, ritual purity, priesthood, moral conduct, sacred time, or covenant land.

Then ask how the New Testament treats that category. Hebrews is especially important for sacrifice and priesthood; Mark 7 and Acts 10 matter for food and purity; 1 Peter uses holiness language; Jesus cites neighbor-love.

This approach avoids two errors: treating Leviticus as irrelevant, or applying Israel’s ritual code directly without regard to fulfillment in Christ.

READING THIS BOOK WITH THE REST OF SCRIPTURE

A strong reading of Leviticus moves in three directions.

FIRST: READ BACKWARD.

Ask what earlier Scripture this book assumes. Covenant promises, creation, Exodus, law, priesthood, kingship, wisdom, and earlier family history often give later passages their meaning.

SECOND: READ FORWARD.

Ask what problems remain unresolved. Does the book achieve final rest? Does the king remain faithful? Does sacrifice permanently remove sin? Does wisdom conquer death? Does reform change the heart? The unresolved questions become part of the Bible’s forward movement.

THIRD: READ CANONICALLY.

Follow how later biblical writers actually use the book. This is more reliable than inventing symbolic connections. When the New Testament quotes, echoes, or develops a passage, pay attention to both continuity and transformation.

This three-direction method helps Christian readers avoid two mistakes: treating the Old Testament as if Christ were irrelevant, or treating every Old Testament detail as a secret code whose only purpose was to predict Jesus.

HOW THIS BOOK POINTS FORWARD TO CHRIST

Hebrews presents Jesus as the greater high priest who enters the true sanctuary once for all.

Christ’s sacrifice fulfills and surpasses the repeated animal sacrifices.

The Day of Atonement gives Christians language for cleansing, access, and sin-bearing.

Jesus quotes Leviticus 19:18 as the second great commandment.

Luke 4’s proclamation of release also resonates with Jubilee themes.

Leviticus therefore becomes far more understandable when read through priesthood, atonement, holiness, and Christ’s fulfillment.

DIFFICULT PASSAGES AND READING CAUTIONS

“ABOMINATION” TEXTS — Read sexual laws within covenant, holiness, and ancient context rather than isolating one English word as a complete theology.

CLEAN/UNCLEAN — Ritual impurity is not identical to moral guilt.

DISABILITY REGULATIONS — These govern priestly symbolism; they are not statements of lesser human dignity.

SACRIFICE — Avoid simplistic formulas that make every sacrifice function identically.

OLD COVENANT APPLICATION — Christians do not simply reenact Israel’s ritual code. New Testament fulfillment must guide application.
