ABOUT AMOS

Amos was a shepherd and agricultural worker from Judah sent north to prophesy against Israel during a period of prosperity. Wealth had increased, worship was active, and the nation felt secure—but injustice, exploitation, and religious hypocrisy were widespread.

Amos is one of Scripture’s clearest witnesses that God cares about economic and judicial injustice.

Outline:
Amos 1–2 — nations judged, then Israel
Amos 3–6 — Israel’s privilege and guilt
Amos 7–9 — visions, confrontation, judgment, restoration

HOW TO READ THIS BOOK

Read the biblical text before the guide section whenever possible. Pay attention to repeated words, images, accusations, promises, changes in speaker, and how judgment and hope relate. The prophets address real historical situations, but their theological message often reaches beyond the immediate crisis.

1. AMOS 1–2 — THE TRAP CLOSES

Amos begins by announcing judgment on surrounding nations.

Israel’s audience may have approved.

Damascus.
Gaza.
Tyre.
Edom.
Ammon.
Moab.
Judah.

Then Amos turns to Israel.

The rhetorical trap is powerful.

The people who enjoyed hearing judgment against others discover that they too are accountable.

Israel’s sins include selling the poor for debt, sexual exploitation, and corrupted worship.

God’s justice is not tribal.

STUDY QUESTION
Why does Amos begin with the sins of other nations before turning to Israel?

2. AMOS 3 — PRIVILEGE CREATES ACCOUNTABILITY

God says:
“You only have I known of all the families of the earth;
therefore I will punish you.”

That sounds surprising.

Israel might expect:
We are chosen, therefore we are safe.

Amos says:
You are chosen, therefore you are responsible.

Election is vocation, not immunity.

The closer the relationship, the more serious covenant betrayal becomes.

STUDY QUESTION
Why does being chosen increase rather than reduce Israel’s accountability?

3. AMOS 4 — RELIGION WITHOUT RETURN

The wealthy women of Samaria are condemned for luxury built on oppression.

Then God lists disasters that should have awakened Israel:
famine,
drought,
crop failure,
plague.

A refrain repeats:
“Yet you did not return to me.”

The tragedy is not simply suffering.

It is suffering that produces no repentance.

STUDY QUESTION
Why can hardship fail to produce spiritual change?

4. AMOS 5 — “LET JUSTICE ROLL DOWN”

Amos 5 contains some of the Bible’s strongest language against hypocritical worship.

God says:
“I hate, I despise your feasts.”

Why?

Because songs and sacrifices coexist with injustice.

Then comes the famous command:
“Let justice roll down like waters,
and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.”

Biblical worship cannot be isolated from public righteousness.

This does not reduce faith to social activism.

It says the God we worship cares how people are treated.

STUDY QUESTION
Why does God reject technically correct worship when injustice dominates the community?

5. THE DAY OF THE LORD

Israel expected the day of the LORD to mean victory over enemies.

Amos shocks them:
“Why would you have the day of the LORD?”

For unrepentant Israel, the day will be darkness.

Religious people can imagine divine judgment only applying to outsiders.

Amos destroys that illusion.

The Judge of all the earth begins with his own people.

STUDY QUESTION
Why is longing for judgment dangerous if we assume we ourselves need no repentance?

6. AMOS 6 — COMFORTABLE IN ZION

Amos condemns those “at ease.”

The problem is not having a bed, music, food, or wine.

The problem is indifference:
they “are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph.”

Luxury has numbed moral awareness.

Comfort becomes spiritually dangerous when it makes another person’s suffering invisible.

STUDY QUESTION
How can comfort weaken compassion?

7. AMOS 7 — AMAZIAH AND THE UNWELCOME PROPHET

The priest Amaziah tells Amos to leave.

He treats Amos as a professional troublemaker interfering with royal religion.

Amos replies that he was not part of a prophetic career system.

God called him from ordinary work.

The conflict exposes a recurring biblical problem:
religious institutions can become more concerned with stability than truth.

STUDY QUESTION
How can institutional self-protection make religious leaders resistant to prophetic correction?

8. AMOS 8 — A FAMINE OF HEARING

Israel manipulates commerce:
shrinking measures,
raising prices,
selling inferior goods,
exploiting the poor.

Amos announces a coming famine:
not of bread,
but of hearing the words of the LORD.

People who continually reject truth may eventually experience the terror of silence.

God’s word is a gift, not an entitlement.

STUDY QUESTION
Why is the absence of God’s word a form of judgment?

9. AMOS 9 — JUDGMENT AND DAVID’S FALLEN BOOTH

The book ends with unexpected restoration.

God will raise up “the booth of David that is fallen.”

Acts 15 later quotes this passage when the early church discusses Gentiles entering the people of God.

James sees the gathering of the nations into the messianic people as connected with Amos’s restoration promise.

The prophet of severe justice ends with abundant hope.

STUDY QUESTION
Why is restoration through David important after a book focused mostly on Israel’s injustice?

MAJOR THEMES

JUSTICE — God cares about courts, markets, debt, exploitation, and power.
WORSHIP — Religious activity without righteousness becomes offensive.
ELECTION — Chosen people bear greater responsibility.
REPENTANCE — Warning is meant to produce return.
WEALTH — Prosperity can numb conscience.
THE WORD — God’s revelation is a precious gift.
RESTORATION — Judgment does not erase God’s Davidic promise.

HOW THIS BOOK POINTS FORWARD TO CHRIST

Acts 15 uses Amos 9 when explaining the inclusion of Gentiles in the messianic people of God.

Jesus’ ministry also embodies Amos’s insistence that religious life and treatment of the vulnerable cannot be separated.

The Davidic restoration hope reaches toward Christ, the Son of David, whose kingdom gathers people from the nations.

DIFFICULT PASSAGES AND READING CAUTIONS

JUSTICE AND POLITICS — Amos should not be reduced to one modern economic ideology. His categories are covenant righteousness, honest commerce, fair courts, and protection of the vulnerable.

WEALTH — Amos does not teach that every wealthy person is automatically wicked. He condemns wealth joined to exploitation and indifference.

DISASTER — Amos interprets particular historical disasters prophetically. Christians should not assume they can identify God’s precise reason for every modern catastrophe.

WHOLE-BOOK REVIEW

1. Why does Amos begin with foreign nations?
2. What does Amos 3:2 teach about election?
3. Why does God reject Israel’s worship?
4. What kind of justice does Amos demand?
5. What is wrong with being “at ease”?
6. Why does Amaziah oppose Amos?
7. What commercial sins appear in Amos 8?
8. What is the famine of hearing?
9. How does Amos end?
10. How does Acts use Amos 9?

FINAL SYNTHESIS:
Explain why Amos would reject the idea that personal piety and public justice can be separated.
