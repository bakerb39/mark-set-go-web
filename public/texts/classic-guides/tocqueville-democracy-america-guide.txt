DEMOCRACY IN AMERICA
An Independent Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide
Original work by Alexis de Tocqueville

A guide to equality, liberty, associations, local government, religion, majority power, individualism, and democratic culture.

ABOUT THIS GUIDE
This independently written educational guide is designed for serious first reading, rereading, and seminar study. It follows the Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide pattern with focused sections, discussion prompts, section quizzes, Great Ideas, review, and whole-guide quiz hooks.

HOW TO USE IT
Read one substantial section at a time. After each section, use Discuss with reading companion for interpretation and Quiz me for a short comprehension check based on that section. Use the final whole-guide quiz after completing the guide.

AT A GLANCE

Tocqueville treats democracy not merely as elections but as a broad social condition defined by increasing equality of conditions. He studies how that condition reshapes politics, religion, family, ambition, thought, associations, and everyday habits.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Tocqueville treat democracy as a social condition rather than simply a form of government?

EQUALITY OF CONDITIONS

Equality weakens inherited ranks and opens social mobility. It can promote dignity and independence, but it can also generate envy, instability, and a desire for uniformity. Tocqueville thinks the movement toward equality is historically powerful and unlikely to reverse.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why can equality strengthen individual dignity while also increasing social pressure toward sameness?

TOWNSHIPS AND LOCAL SELF-GOVERNMENT

American local institutions train citizens in practical political participation. People learn freedom by governing concrete common affairs. Liberty is therefore sustained by habits and experience, not simply constitutional text.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Tocqueville think local government functions as a school of citizenship?

ASSOCIATIONS

Voluntary associations allow citizens to combine their efforts without depending entirely on the state. Political, religious, commercial, and civic associations counteract isolation and teach cooperation.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How do associations protect liberty in a society where individuals are otherwise increasingly independent and socially equal?

RELIGION

Tocqueville argues that religion can support democratic liberty by establishing moral limits and shared habits while remaining institutionally separate from direct political power. He sees American religion as influential partly because it does not govern the state.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why might religion possess greater social authority when it does not directly control government?

TYRANNY OF THE MAJORITY

A democratic majority can dominate not only law but opinion. Tocqueville fears a social pressure that makes dissent psychologically difficult even without formal censorship.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How can majority opinion threaten freedom even when constitutional procedures are followed?

INDIVIDUALISM

Tocqueville distinguishes individualism from crude selfishness. Democratic people may withdraw into family and private life because old bonds of rank have weakened. The danger is civic passivity rather than simple greed.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does democratic equality make withdrawal into private life especially tempting?

SOFT DESPOTISM

Tocqueville imagines a new kind of despotism: an immense tutelary power that manages citizens’ needs while leaving them formally peaceful and equal. People may surrender political agency not through terror but through dependence and administrative convenience.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why is soft despotism harder to recognize than traditional tyranny?

PRESS, JURIES, AND POLITICAL HABITS

A free press, jury service, elections, and public controversy cultivate habits of participation. Tocqueville’s institutional analysis repeatedly returns to the formation of citizens’ character.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why are civic habits as important as formal legal protections in Tocqueville’s theory?

RACE, SLAVERY, AND THE LIMITS OF AMERICAN DEMOCRACY

Tocqueville directly confronts slavery, racial hierarchy, and the displacement of Native peoples. These realities expose the gap between democratic ideals and actual political membership. His observations are perceptive but also limited by the assumptions of his era.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What does the coexistence of democratic equality and racial domination reveal about the limits of political ideals?

DEMOCRATIC CULTURE

Equality shapes literature, science, industry, ambition, manners, family relations, and intellectual life. Democratic societies often favor practical results, accessibility, and broad participation over aristocratic refinement.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How might the same democratic tendencies produce both cultural vitality and pressures toward mediocrity?

LEGACY

Democracy in America remains influential because it analyzes tensions that persist: equality versus liberty, centralization versus participation, privacy versus citizenship, and majority rule versus independence of mind.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which of Tocqueville’s democratic dangers seems most relevant to a highly networked modern society?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the central problem of the work in one sentence.
2. Identify the author’s strongest argument, insight, or dramatic achievement.
3. Distinguish what the work describes from what it endorses.
4. Track the work’s major conceptual, causal, or character relationships.
5. Identify the historical assumptions on which the work depends.
6. Explain one major term, image, or conflict in the work’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection or criticism that can be made.
9. Identify one idea or achievement that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which one claim from this work most changes the way you understand its subject?

DEEPER READING LABS

LAB 1 — ARGUMENT OR PLOT MAP
Trace the central movement of the work. For philosophy and political thought, map the thesis and its strongest supports. For literature, map the decisive choices, reversals, and consequences.

LAB 2 — KEY DISTINCTIONS
Identify five distinctions, contrasts, or oppositions the work relies on. Explain what becomes harder to understand if each one is blurred.

LAB 3 — HISTORICAL TEST
Choose one historical assumption, institution, or social practice in the work and examine how much the work depends on it.

LAB 4 — STRONGEST OBJECTION
Construct the strongest objection you can to the work’s central vision, then answer it using evidence from the work itself.

LAB 5 — GREAT BOOKS CONNECTION
Compare this work with at least two earlier authors on freedom, morality, authority, knowledge, society, religion, nature, or human character.
