An Independent Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide
Original work by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay

A focused guide to union, faction, federalism, separation of powers, checks and balances, representation, executive power, the judiciary, and constitutional ratification.

This independently written educational guide is designed for serious first reading, rereading, and seminar study. It follows the Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide pattern with focused sections, discussion prompts, section quizzes, Great Ideas, review, and whole-guide quiz hooks.

AT A GLANCE

The Federalist Papers are eighty-five essays written to persuade New Yorkers to ratify the proposed Constitution. They are advocacy documents, but they also provide one of the most influential explanations of the Constitution’s design.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How should the essays be read differently because they are both political persuasion and constitutional theory?

THE CRISIS OF THE CONFEDERATION

Hamilton and his coauthors portray the Articles of Confederation as too weak to secure revenue, regulate common interests, enforce national decisions, or protect the Union from foreign and domestic danger.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which failures of the Confederation require stronger national authority rather than merely better cooperation among states?

UNION AND SECURITY

The early papers argue that a durable union reduces interstate rivalry, foreign manipulation, and fragmented defense. Jay and Hamilton treat geopolitical security as a constitutional question.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does the structure of domestic government affect a nation’s vulnerability to foreign powers?

FEDERALIST 10 — FACTION

Madison defines faction as a group united by passion or interest adverse to the rights of others or the permanent interests of the community. The causes of faction cannot be removed without destroying liberty, so the task is to control effects.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Madison prefer controlling the effects of faction to eliminating its causes?

FEDERALIST 51 — CHECKS AND BALANCES

Because rulers are not angels, constitutional design must supply internal checks. Departments need constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachment. Power is checked by power rather than by assuming virtue alone.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does Federalist 51 turn self-interest from a political danger into part of the constitutional defense?

FEDERALISM

Authority is divided between national and state governments. The authors argue that this division adds security for liberty while permitting national action on genuinely national problems.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What kinds of problems are best handled nationally, and what kinds benefit from state authority?

REPRESENTATION AND THE EXTENDED REPUBLIC

Representation is not merely a substitute for direct democracy. Madison hopes representatives can refine public views, while the extended republic multiplies interests and makes durable oppressive majorities harder to organize.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
When does representation improve public judgment, and when can it distance government from the people?

THE EXECUTIVE

Hamilton’s defense of executive energy emphasizes unity, duration, adequate powers, and responsibility. A vigorous executive can act decisively while a single identifiable officeholder can also be held accountable clearly.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Hamilton think energy and accountability can reinforce each other?

THE SENATE

The Senate is defended as a source of stability, continuity, and deliberation. Longer terms can insulate some decisions from immediate passions while sharing responsibility for treaties and appointments.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What democratic risks arise when stability is purchased through insulation from immediate public opinion?

FEDERALIST 78 — THE JUDICIARY

Hamilton calls the judiciary the least dangerous branch because it controls neither sword nor purse. Judicial independence protects legal judgment from transient pressure, and his discussion anticipates judicial review.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does constitutional supremacy require some institution capable of refusing effect to unconstitutional ordinary laws?

THE BILL OF RIGHTS DEBATE

Federalist 84 argues that a bill of rights may be unnecessary or risky because the federal government has only delegated powers. The eventual Bill of Rights shows how ratification politics changed the constitutional settlement.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What does this debate reveal about the difference between constitutional design and constitutional settlement?

GREAT IDEAS AND LEGACY

The essays remain central because they connect mechanisms to recurring problems: faction, ambition, scale, representation, energy, federalism, and judicial independence. They should also be read alongside Anti-Federalist criticism.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which Federalist arguments depend most on assumptions about human nature?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the central problem of the work in one sentence.
2. Identify the author’s strongest argument.
3. Distinguish description from prescription.
4. Track the major causal or conceptual relationships.
5. Identify the historical assumptions the argument depends on.
6. Explain one major term in the author’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection to the work.
9. Identify one idea that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which one claim from this work most changes the way you understand its subject?

DEEPER READING LABS

LAB 1 — ARGUMENT MAP
Write the central thesis, then list the three strongest supporting arguments and evidence for each.

LAB 2 — KEY DISTINCTIONS
Identify five distinctions the author relies on and explain what breaks if each is ignored.

LAB 3 — HISTORICAL TEST
Choose one historical example and ask whether it really proves what the author says it proves.

LAB 4 — STRONGEST OBJECTION
Construct the strongest objection you can, then answer it using the work itself.

LAB 5 — GREAT BOOKS CONNECTION
Compare the work with at least two earlier authors on authority, knowledge, morality, politics, science, or human nature.
