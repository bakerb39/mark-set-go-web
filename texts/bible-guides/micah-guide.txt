ABOUT MICAH

Micah prophesied in Judah during the eighth century BC, overlapping with Isaiah. He addresses corrupt leaders, wealthy land-grabbers, false prophets, injustice, coming judgment, and extraordinary hope centered on a future ruler from Bethlehem.

Micah alternates between judgment and salvation. The book’s name sounds like the question at its climax: “Who is a God like you?”

Outline:
Micah 1–3 — sin and corrupt leadership
Micah 4–5 — future kingdom and ruler
Micah 6 — covenant lawsuit and true obedience
Micah 7 — lament, hope, forgiveness

HOW TO READ THIS BOOK

Read the biblical text before the guide section whenever possible. Pay attention to repeated words, images, accusations, promises, changes in speaker, and how judgment and hope relate. The prophets address real historical situations, but their theological message often reaches beyond the immediate crisis.

1. MICAH 1 — THE LORD COMES AS JUDGE

Micah begins with God descending in judgment.

Samaria and Jerusalem are both implicated.

The prophet does not speak as a detached observer.

He laments.

Biblical prophets can announce judgment while grieving over the people who face it.

Truth and compassion belong together.

STUDY QUESTION
Why is grief an appropriate response even when judgment is deserved?

2. MICAH 2 — LAND-GRABBING AND POWER

Powerful people lie awake planning how to seize fields and houses.

They carry out the scheme because “it is in the power of their hand.”

Micah exposes a basic temptation:
ability becomes permission.

If I can do it,
I may assume I have the right to do it.

But God judges the use of power.

Property, law, and economic advantage do not excuse exploitation.

STUDY QUESTION
What is the difference between having legal power and having moral right?

3. MICAH 3 — CORRUPT LEADERS

Rulers “hate the good and love the evil.”

Prophets preach peace when they are paid and hostility when they are not.

Priests teach for a price.

The leaders assume:
“Is not the LORD in the midst of us?”

They use religious identity as insulation from accountability.

Micah says Jerusalem will fall.

No institution is protected when leaders systematically corrupt its purpose.

STUDY QUESTION
What warning does Micah give leaders who confuse religious status with divine approval?

4. MICAH 4 — SWORDS INTO PLOWSHARES

Micah shares the great vision also found in Isaiah 2.

Nations stream to the mountain of the LORD.

They learn God’s ways.

Swords become plowshares.
Spears become pruning hooks.

People sit under vine and fig tree without fear.

Biblical peace is not merely absence of conflict.

It is ordered life under God’s justice, where people can live securely.

STUDY QUESTION
Why does lasting peace require people to learn God’s ways rather than merely sign treaties?

5. MICAH 5 — THE RULER FROM BETHLEHEM

Micah promises a ruler from Bethlehem Ephrathah.

Bethlehem is small.

Yet from it comes the ruler whose origins are described in elevated language.

He will shepherd God’s flock.

He will be their peace.

Matthew quotes Micah 5 when the Magi ask where the Messiah is to be born.

The contrast is deeply biblical:
great salvation comes from a place the world considers insignificant.

STUDY QUESTION
What does Bethlehem teach about how God often works through what appears small?

6. MICAH 6 — “WHAT DOES THE LORD REQUIRE?”

Micah pictures a covenant lawsuit.

God asks what he has done to burden his people.

He reminds them of rescue.

Then the people ask:
What should we bring?
Sacrifices?
Thousands of rams?
Rivers of oil?

Micah answers:
“He has told you, O man, what is good.”

Do justice.
Love mercy.
Walk humbly with your God.

This is not a rejection of grace or worship.

It is a rejection of the idea that religious offerings can purchase God while the heart remains unjust and proud.

STUDY QUESTION
How do justice, mercy, and humility hold together as a picture of faithful life?

7. MICAH 7 — “WHO IS A GOD LIKE YOU?”

The book ends with hope.

Micah waits for God.

Then he praises God’s incomparable mercy:
Who is a God like you,
pardoning iniquity?

God casts sins into the depths of the sea.

He remains faithful to promises made to Abraham and Jacob.

The final answer to the book’s sin is not human moral achievement.

It is covenant mercy.

STUDY QUESTION
Why is forgiveness, rather than judgment, the final word of Micah?

MAJOR THEMES

JUSTICE — God judges exploitation and corrupt courts.
LEADERSHIP — Power is accountable to God.
PEACE — True peace grows from God’s righteous rule.
MESSIAH — A shepherd-ruler comes from Bethlehem.
HUMILITY — Faithful life is not religious performance.
MERCY — God pardons and remains covenant-faithful.
HOPE — Judgment is not the end.

HOW THIS BOOK POINTS FORWARD TO CHRIST

Matthew 2 explicitly connects Micah 5 with Jesus’ birth in Bethlehem.

Jesus is the Davidic shepherd-ruler who brings peace.

Micah 6:8 also resonates with Jesus’ emphasis on the weightier matters of justice, mercy, and faithfulness.

Micah ends by grounding hope in God’s promise to Abraham, connecting the book to the covenant storyline fulfilled in Christ’s blessing to the nations.

DIFFICULT PASSAGES AND READING CAUTIONS

MICAH 6:8 — This verse should not be turned into salvation by moral effort. In context, faithful conduct grows out of covenant relationship and God’s prior acts of grace.

PEACE PROPHECY — Micah’s peace vision is not a claim that faithful people can create final world peace through human effort alone. It points to God’s kingdom.

BETHLEHEM — Matthew’s use is explicitly messianic. Christians should read Micah 5 in both its original royal hope and New Testament fulfillment.

WHOLE-BOOK REVIEW

1. What sins does Micah condemn in leaders?
2. How do the powerful misuse land?
3. What is false about the prophets in chapter 3?
4. What does “swords into plowshares” mean?
5. Why is Bethlehem significant?
6. What does Micah 6:8 require?
7. Why are sacrifices insufficient?
8. How does the book end?
9. How does Matthew use Micah?
10. What does Micah teach about God’s character?

FINAL SYNTHESIS:
Explain how Micah moves from corrupt power to a humble ruler and from deserved judgment to incomparable mercy.
