ABOUT OBADIAH

Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament, but its message is concentrated: pride, violence, betrayal, the day of the LORD, and the eventual triumph of God’s kingdom.

The book addresses Edom, Israel’s neighboring people descended from Esau. Edom is condemned for standing aloof—and apparently participating—when Jerusalem was attacked.

The theological issue is not ancient ethnic rivalry for its own sake. It is the moral horror of rejoicing over a brother’s disaster and exploiting vulnerability.

HOW TO READ THIS BOOK

Read the biblical text before the guide section whenever possible. Pay attention to repeated words, images, accusations, promises, changes in speaker, and how judgment and hope relate. The prophets address real historical situations, but their theological message often reaches beyond the immediate crisis.

1. EDOM AND ISRAEL

Genesis traces Israel through Jacob and Edom through Esau.

That family connection makes Edom’s betrayal especially serious.

Obadiah calls them “your brother Jacob.”

The language reminds readers that political enemies may still carry relational obligations.

Ancient hostility does not erase moral responsibility.

2. THE PRIDE OF EDOM

Edom’s mountain strongholds seemed secure.

The nation says in its heart:
“Who will bring me down to the ground?”

God answers:
“I will bring you down.”

Pride is not merely feeling good about achievement.

Biblical pride treats creaturely strength as self-created and ultimate.

Geography,
military advantage,
wealth,
or intelligence
cannot make a people invulnerable to God.

STUDY QUESTION
What forms of security most easily produce false invulnerability today?

3. THE SIN OF STANDING ALOOF

Obadiah says Edom stood aloof on the day strangers carried off Jerusalem’s wealth.

They looked on.
They rejoiced.
They entered the city.
They looted.
They cut off fugitives.

The progression is chilling.

Sin begins with emotional distance and develops into active exploitation.

The book teaches that neutrality toward another’s destruction can become complicity.

STUDY QUESTION
When does “staying out of it” become moral failure?

4. THE DAY OF THE LORD

Obadiah widens from Edom to “all the nations.”

“As you have done, it shall be done to you.”

This is moral reversal.

Violence returns on the violent.
Pride is humbled.
God’s kingdom prevails.

The book’s final sentence is:
“the kingdom shall be the LORD’s.”

That is the true center of Obadiah.

Edom is temporary.
God’s reign is not.

STUDY QUESTION
Why does Obadiah move from one nation’s judgment to God’s universal kingdom?

MAJOR THEMES

PRIDE — False security blinds people to dependence on God.
BROTHERHOOD — Historical conflict does not erase moral obligation.
COMPLICITY — Watching, rejoicing, and exploiting can become stages of shared guilt.
JUSTICE — God’s judgment has a moral correspondence.
KINGDOM — The book ends with God’s rule, not Edom’s destruction.

HOW THIS BOOK POINTS FORWARD TO CHRIST

Obadiah’s closing declaration—“the kingdom shall be the LORD’s”—fits the Bible’s larger kingdom hope fulfilled in Christ.

Jesus announces the kingdom of God and teaches love even toward enemies.

Obadiah’s reversal of proud powers also participates in the biblical pattern culminating in Christ’s final judgment and kingdom.

DIFFICULT PASSAGES AND READING CAUTIONS

EDOM AND MODERN PEOPLES — Do not map ancient Edom directly onto a modern ethnic or political group.

RETRIBUTION — “As you have done” teaches divine justice, not personal permission for revenge.

SHORT BOOK, LARGE CLAIMS — Obadiah’s brevity should not tempt readers to reduce it to “God hates Edom.” Its real concern is pride, violence, complicity, justice, and kingdom.

WHOLE-BOOK REVIEW

1. Who were the Edomites related to?
2. What made Edom feel secure?
3. What did Edom do during Jerusalem’s disaster?
4. Why is standing aloof condemned?
5. What is the day of the LORD?
6. What principle appears in verse 15?
7. How does the book end?
8. What does Obadiah teach about pride?

FINAL SYNTHESIS:
Explain how Obadiah turns a local act of betrayal into a universal lesson about pride and God’s kingdom.
