EXODUS
Mark, Set, Go! Bible Guide

ABOUT THIS BOOK

Exodus tells how God rescues Israel from slavery in Egypt, forms them into a covenant people, gives them his law, and comes to dwell among them. It is one of the foundational books for understanding salvation in the rest of Scripture.

The book moves from oppression to deliverance, from deliverance to covenant, and from covenant to presence. Pharaoh asks, “Who is the LORD?” The whole book answers that question.

Exodus is therefore not merely about leaving Egypt. It is about belonging to God.

BOOK STRUCTURE

Exodus 1–6 — slavery, Moses, and God’s name
Exodus 7–12 — plagues and Passover
Exodus 13–18 — sea crossing and wilderness
Exodus 19–24 — Sinai covenant
Exodus 25–31 — tabernacle instructions
Exodus 32–34 — golden calf, intercession, covenant renewal
Exodus 35–40 — tabernacle built and glory fills it

HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE

Read the biblical text first whenever possible. Then use the guide to trace the argument, narrative movement, theology, historical context, difficult passages, and connections to the rest of Scripture. The goal is serious understanding without turning the guide into a technical commentary.

1. EXODUS 1 — OPPRESSION AND THE FEAR OF GOD

A new Pharaoh fears Israel’s growth and turns fear into oppression. Forced labor escalates into attempted genocide.

Yet the Hebrew midwives fear God more than Pharaoh and refuse his command.

This contrast introduces one of Exodus’s central questions: Who deserves ultimate allegiance?

Pharaoh claims authority over life and death. The midwives quietly deny that claim.

Biblical courage often begins not with public spectacle but with ordinary people refusing evil.

What does the contrast between fearing Pharaoh and fearing God teach about moral courage?

2. EXODUS 2–4 — MOSES: CALLING AND RELUCTANCE

Moses is born under death threat, raised in Pharaoh’s household, flees after killing an Egyptian, and spends years in Midian.

At the burning bush, God calls him.

Moses responds with objection after objection:
Who am I?
What is your name?
What if they do not believe me?
I am not eloquent.
Please send someone else.

God’s answer is not that Moses secretly has enough self-confidence.

“I will be with you.”

Calling rests on God’s presence more than personal adequacy.

3. EXODUS 3 — THE NAME OF GOD

God identifies himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and reveals the divine name YHWH.

“I AM WHO I AM” has generated enormous theological reflection.

At minimum, the passage communicates God’s self-existence, freedom, faithfulness, and presence.

God is not one deity among others who can be manipulated.

He is the covenant God who sees suffering, remembers promise, and acts.

The divine name becomes central to Israel’s worship and to later biblical theology.

4. EXODUS 5–11 — PHARAOH, PLAGUES, AND HARDNESS OF HEART

The confrontation with Pharaoh escalates through signs and plagues.

The plagues demonstrate that Egypt’s power, gods, economy, and king cannot resist the LORD.

Pharaoh’s hardened heart is described in several ways:
Pharaoh hardens his heart.
His heart is hardened.
God hardens his heart.

The narrative holds human responsibility and divine sovereignty together.

Pharaoh repeatedly resists, bargains, relents temporarily, and returns to defiance.

The deeper issue is not stubborn personality but refusal to recognize God’s authority.

5. EXODUS 12 — PASSOVER

The final plague brings death to Egypt’s firstborn.

Israel is commanded to sacrifice a lamb, mark the doorposts with blood, eat in readiness, and remember the night permanently.

Passover becomes one of Israel’s defining acts of memory.

Salvation is both:
deliverance from judgment,
and liberation from slavery.

The meal teaches later generations to say not merely, “God rescued them,” but “God rescued us.”

Biblical faith is sustained through remembered salvation.

6. EXODUS 13–15 — THE SEA CROSSING

Israel leaves Egypt but quickly becomes trapped between Pharaoh’s army and the sea.

The people panic.

God opens a way.

The sea crossing is the decisive act of liberation.

Israel does not defeat Egypt militarily. Salvation comes through God’s intervention.

Exodus 15 responds with song.

Worship follows deliverance.

The song celebrates God as warrior, king, and redeemer.

The Bible’s pattern is important:
God acts,
then his people sing.

7. EXODUS 16–18 — WILDERNESS: HUNGER, WATER, AND LEARNING TRUST

Freedom does not instantly create maturity.

Israel complains about food and water.

God provides manna, quail, and water.

The wilderness becomes a school of dependence.

Manna cannot be hoarded safely.

Daily provision trains daily trust.

Jethro later advises Moses to share leadership.

Exodus therefore connects spiritual dependence with practical organization.

Trust in God does not eliminate wise delegation.

8. EXODUS 19 — A KINGDOM OF PRIESTS

At Sinai, God tells Israel:
“You shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation.”

Election now becomes mission.

Priests represent God to people and people before God.

Israel’s covenant identity is therefore not private privilege.

They are called to embody God’s holiness among the nations.

The New Testament later applies similar language to the church.

9. EXODUS 20 — THE TEN COMMANDMENTS

The commandments begin with grace:
“I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt.”

Only then come commands.

That order matters.

Israel obeys as a rescued people, not in order to persuade God to rescue them.

The commandments order:
worship,
speech,
time,
family,
life,
sexuality,
property,
truth,
and desire.

The final command against coveting moves morality inward.

God cares not only about external acts but disordered desire.

10. EXODUS 21–24 — COVENANT LIFE

The laws following the Ten Commandments apply covenant principles to ancient Israel’s social world.

They address servants, violence, property, restitution, justice, lending, courts, and treatment of foreigners.

Readers must distinguish:
the enduring moral principles,
Israel’s ancient civil covenant,
and direct Christian application.

One repeated concern is protection against abuse by the powerful.

God reminds Israel:
“You were strangers in Egypt.”

Memory of oppression should produce compassion.

11. EXODUS 25–31 — THE TABERNACLE

The long tabernacle instructions can feel surprising after dramatic deliverance.

But the theological goal of Exodus is not merely getting Israel out of Egypt.

It is God dwelling among them.

The tabernacle uses rich symbolism:
ark,
lampstand,
bread,
altar,
priesthood,
holy place,
Most Holy Place.

The structure teaches that God is present, holy, and graciously accessible through the means he provides.

12. EXODUS 32 — THE GOLDEN CALF

While Moses is on Sinai, Israel builds a golden calf.

The speed of the collapse is shocking.

The people who saw the sea divided now make a visible god.

Idolatry is often an attempt to make God manageable.

Moses intercedes.

He appeals to:
God’s reputation,
God’s promises,
and God’s covenant.

The chapter reveals both the seriousness of sin and the importance of intercession.

13. EXODUS 33–34 — “SHOW ME YOUR GLORY”

After the golden calf, Moses asks for God’s presence.

Then he asks:
“Show me your glory.”

God reveals his name and character:
merciful,
gracious,
slow to anger,
abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness,

This becomes one of the Old Testament’s most important descriptions of God.

The glory of God is not raw power alone.

His glory is revealed through holy, merciful, faithful character.

HISTORICAL, LITERARY, AND READING CONTEXT

HISTORICAL AND LITERARY CONTEXT

Exodus is set against Egypt’s use of forced labor and royal power. The text does not identify the Pharaoh by name, and attempts to fix the Exodus to one precise Egyptian ruler remain debated. For reading the book well, the more important literary point is that Pharaoh represents an empire claiming ownership over people who belong to God.

Exodus also deliberately continues Genesis. Genesis ends with Joseph’s coffin in Egypt; Exodus begins with Israel multiplying there. The promise to Abraham—offspring, land, blessing—now appears threatened by slavery. God’s repeated statement that he “remembered his covenant” does not mean he had forgotten. Biblical remembrance means covenant commitment moving into action.

The book’s geography is also theological. Israel moves from Egypt to the sea, through wilderness, to Sinai. The destination is not yet Canaan. Sinai comes first because redemption creates a people who must learn worship, covenant, and holiness before entering land.

READING EXODUS AS A WHOLE

Watch the repeated phrase “that you may know that I am the LORD.” The plagues reveal God to Pharaoh, Egypt, and Israel.

Also track the movement of service. Israel begins as slaves serving Pharaoh. After deliverance, they are called to serve/worship the LORD. Biblical freedom is therefore not autonomy; it is liberation from a false master for life under the true God.

Finally, notice how often God’s presence drives the story: burning bush, cloud, fire, mountain, tabernacle, glory. Exodus is fundamentally a book about the presence of God with a redeemed people.

READING THIS BOOK WITH THE REST OF SCRIPTURE

A strong reading of Exodus moves in three directions.

FIRST: READ BACKWARD.

Ask what earlier Scripture this book assumes. Covenant promises, creation, Exodus, law, priesthood, kingship, wisdom, and earlier family history often give later passages their meaning.

SECOND: READ FORWARD.

Ask what problems remain unresolved. Does the book achieve final rest? Does the king remain faithful? Does sacrifice permanently remove sin? Does wisdom conquer death? Does reform change the heart? The unresolved questions become part of the Bible’s forward movement.

THIRD: READ CANONICALLY.

Follow how later biblical writers actually use the book. This is more reliable than inventing symbolic connections. When the New Testament quotes, echoes, or develops a passage, pay attention to both continuity and transformation.

This three-direction method helps Christian readers avoid two mistakes: treating the Old Testament as if Christ were irrelevant, or treating every Old Testament detail as a secret code whose only purpose was to predict Jesus.

HOW THIS BOOK POINTS FORWARD TO CHRIST

Jesus is presented through Exodus-shaped imagery throughout the New Testament.

John the Baptist identifies Jesus as the Lamb of God.
Paul calls Christ “our Passover.”
The Last Supper occurs in Passover context.
Jesus leads a greater exodus from slavery to sin and death.
Baptism is compared typologically with Israel passing through the sea.
The church is called a royal priesthood.
John’s Gospel presents Jesus as the true tabernacle of God’s presence.
Revelation sings songs that echo Moses and the Exodus victory.

Exodus therefore provides some of Christianity’s basic vocabulary for salvation.

DIFFICULT PASSAGES AND READING CAUTIONS

HARDENING PHARAOH — Scripture simultaneously affirms Pharaoh’s responsibility and God’s sovereignty. Avoid solving the tension by denying one side.

THE PLAGUES — The suffering involved is severe. Read them as acts of judgment within the story’s confrontation between oppressive empire and God’s liberating rule, not as entertainment.

SLAVERY LAWS — Ancient Israelite servitude differs in important ways from modern race-based chattel slavery, but the laws still reflect an ancient social world rather than the final biblical ideal. Do not use them to defend modern slavery.

PASSOVER AND CHRIST — Christian typology should deepen respect for Passover’s Jewish meaning rather than erase it.

THE LAW — Christians disagree about how Mosaic civil, ceremonial, and moral laws relate to the church. Read individual commands within covenant context and through New Testament teaching.
