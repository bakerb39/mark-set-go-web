ABOUT NAHUM

Nahum announces the fall of Nineveh, capital of Assyria. Where Jonah emphasized God’s mercy toward repentant Nineveh, Nahum addresses a later Assyria known for imperial brutality.

The book is uncomfortable because it celebrates the collapse of an oppressor. It should not be read as personal revenge poetry. It is theological assurance that violent empires are not permanent and that God hears the suffering of those crushed by them.

HOW TO READ THIS BOOK

Read the biblical text before the guide section whenever possible. Pay attention to repeated words, images, accusations, promises, changes in speaker, and how judgment and hope relate. The prophets address real historical situations, but their theological message often reaches beyond the immediate crisis.

1. THE LORD IS JEALOUS AND AVENGING

Nahum begins with language of divine jealousy and vengeance.

Biblical jealousy is covenantal: God refuses to treat evil and idolatry as trivial.

Divine vengeance is not human spite. It is justice belonging to the Judge.

The same opening also says:
“The LORD is slow to anger and great in power.”

Patience should not be confused with indifference.

STUDY QUESTION
How does divine vengeance differ from human revenge?

2. NAHUM 1 — GOOD NEWS FOR THE OPPRESSED

In the middle of judgment language comes:
“The LORD is good,
a stronghold in the day of trouble.”

The fall of Assyria is good news to those Assyria terrorized.

This helps explain why biblical judgment can be celebrated.

For victims, the end of oppression is liberation.

STUDY QUESTION
Why can judgment on an oppressor be experienced as salvation by victims?

3. NAHUM 2 — NINEVEH FALLS

Nahum poetically describes the city’s collapse.

The empire that plundered others is plundered.

The lion imagery mocks Assyria’s self-image.

Power built through violence eventually becomes vulnerable to violence.

The chapter is not a geopolitical prediction for modern use. It is a moral exposure of imperial arrogance.

STUDY QUESTION
What makes violent power unstable even when it appears overwhelming?

4. NAHUM 3 — “WHO HAS NOT FELT YOUR ENDLESS CRUELTY?”

Nineveh is condemned for bloodshed, lies, exploitation, and cruelty.

The book ends with the nations applauding its fall.

That ending is severe.

But the final question explains why:
Who has not felt your endless cruelty?

Nahum speaks from the perspective of people who have watched evil go unpunished for too long.

STUDY QUESTION
How can Christians affirm justice for victims without cultivating hatred?

MAJOR THEMES

DIVINE JUSTICE — God does not ignore oppression forever.
PATIENCE — Slow anger is not moral indifference.
EMPIRE — Violent powers are temporary.
REFUGE — God is a stronghold for those who trust him.
REVERSAL — The plunderer is plundered.

HOW THIS BOOK POINTS FORWARD TO CHRIST

Nahum’s “good news” language about the defeat of oppression contributes to the larger biblical pattern of gospel as announcement of God’s victorious reign.

Christ’s kingdom finally defeats evil without becoming another violent empire.

Revelation similarly celebrates the fall of “Babylon,” the symbolic city of oppressive world power.

DIFFICULT PASSAGES AND READING CAUTIONS

CELEBRATING DESTRUCTION — Nahum should not authorize personal hatred or delight in suffering. The celebration is the end of systemic cruelty.

JONAH AND NAHUM — The books are not contradictory. Jonah shows God’s willingness to forgive repentance. Nahum shows that later persistent violence is still judged.

MODERN NATIONS — Do not identify Nineveh simplistically with a current country.
