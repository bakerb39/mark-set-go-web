ABOUT EZEKIEL

Ezekiel is one of the Bible’s strangest and most visually powerful prophets.

He sees:
wheels within wheels,
living creatures,
the glory of God,
symbolic actions,
a valley of dry bones,
a restored temple,
and a river bringing life.

Ezekiel was a priest and prophet among the Jewish exiles in Babylon. Jerusalem and its temple still existed when his ministry began, but many exiles wrongly assumed disaster would soon end.

Ezekiel announces something harder:
Jerusalem will fall.

Then, after judgment, the book turns toward restoration:
a new shepherd,
a new heart,
a new spirit,
resurrection imagery,
reunited people,
and God dwelling among them.

Large outline:

Ezekiel 1–3 — call and vision
Ezekiel 4–24 — judgment on Jerusalem
Ezekiel 25–32 — nations judged
Ezekiel 33–39 — restoration and new life
Ezekiel 40–48 — temple, land, river, and God’s presence

1. EZEKIEL 1 — THE GLORY OF GOD IN EXILE

Ezekiel sees a storm, living creatures, wheels, fire, and a throne.

The imagery is overwhelming.

The point is not to satisfy curiosity about heavenly machinery.

The vision communicates divine mobility and majesty.

God’s glory is not trapped in Jerusalem.

Ezekiel is in Babylon.

Yet God is there.

This would have been enormously important to exiles.

They had lost:
land,
security,
and eventually the temple.

But they had not escaped God’s presence.

2. EZEKIEL 2–3 — THE WATCHMAN

God commissions Ezekiel to speak whether people listen or refuse.

He eats a scroll containing God’s words.

The scroll tastes sweet.

The image says:
the prophet must receive God’s word personally before speaking it publicly.

Ezekiel is also called a watchman.

A watchman sees danger and warns.

Faithfulness is measured by giving the warning, not by controlling the response.

This is an important lesson for Christian witness:
we are responsible for truthfulness,
not for manipulating outcomes.

3. EZEKIEL’S SYMBOLIC ACTIONS

Ezekiel acts out messages.

He lies on his side.
He builds a model siege.
He cuts his hair.
He packs baggage like an exile.

These actions are prophetic drama.

They make the message visible.

God is trying to break through a people’s denial.

Sometimes truth must be shown as well as spoken.

The actions are intentionally uncomfortable because the coming judgment is uncomfortable.

4. EZEKIEL 8–11 — IDOLATRY IN THE TEMPLE AND THE DEPARTURE OF GLORY

Ezekiel sees corrupt worship inside Jerusalem’s temple.

This is devastating.

The place meant for God’s presence has become filled with idolatry.

Then God’s glory departs.

The departure happens in stages.

It is as though the book wants us to feel the tragedy.

The temple building cannot guarantee God’s presence while the people reject God.

Yet chapter 11 includes hope.

God promises to be a sanctuary to the exiles.

Even outside Jerusalem, God can be present with his people.

5. EZEKIEL 16 — JERUSALEM’S UNFAITHFULNESS

Ezekiel 16 uses shocking marriage imagery.

Jerusalem is pictured as someone rescued, loved, adorned, and then unfaithful.

The language is intentionally disturbing.

The chapter communicates:
grace received,
privilege abused,
and covenant betrayal.

Readers should handle the imagery carefully, especially because sexualized violence in prophetic metaphors can be painful for modern readers.

The theological point is not permission for human abuse.

It is the seriousness of covenant unfaithfulness.

Why does Scripture sometimes use emotionally shocking imagery to describe idolatry?

6. EZEKIEL 18 — PERSONAL RESPONSIBILITY

A proverb circulates:
“The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children’s teeth are set on edge.”

People feel trapped by previous generations.

Ezekiel emphasizes individual moral responsibility.

“The soul who sins shall die.”

A righteous child is not condemned simply because his father was wicked.

A wicked person who turns can live.

A righteous person who abandons righteousness cannot rely on the past.

This is a powerful biblical rejection of fatalism.

Family and social consequences are real.

But a person is not morally doomed by ancestry.

7. EZEKIEL 24 — JERUSALEM FALLS AND THE PROPHET SUFFERS

The siege begins.

Then Ezekiel’s wife dies.

He is told to embody the stunned grief that will overwhelm the people when the temple falls.

This is one of Ezekiel’s most difficult passages.

The prophet’s personal life becomes part of his message in an extreme way.

Readers should not turn this unique prophetic sign into a general model for how God treats bereaved people.

The passage communicates the catastrophic emotional shock of losing what Judah assumed could never be lost.

Why must unique prophetic sign-acts not be turned into general rules about God’s dealings with believers?

8. EZEKIEL 25–32 — THE NATIONS ARE ACCOUNTABLE TOO

Ammon,
Moab,
Edom,
Philistia,
Tyre,
Sidon,
and Egypt
come under judgment.

Judah is not singled out because other nations are righteous.

God judges pride, cruelty, violence, and arrogance everywhere.

TYRE

Tyre’s wealth and pride receive extended treatment.

Ezekiel describes the danger of becoming intoxicated with prosperity.

The richer and more successful a power becomes, the easier it becomes to imagine it is untouchable.

Scripture repeatedly says:
no human power is ultimate.

9. EZEKIEL 33 — THE WATCHMAN AGAIN

After Jerusalem falls, Ezekiel’s ministry changes.

The judgment he predicted has occurred.

Now restoration becomes central.

The watchman theme returns.

God says he does not delight in the death of the wicked.

“Turn back, turn back from your evil ways.”

This is important for understanding biblical judgment.

God’s warnings are not evidence that he enjoys destruction.

They are calls to repentance.

10. EZEKIEL 34 — BAD SHEPHERDS AND THE ONE SHEPHERD

Israel’s leaders are condemned as shepherds who feed themselves instead of the flock.

They exploit rather than protect.

God says:
“I myself will search for my sheep.”

Then God promises:
“I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David.”

This is striking.

God himself will shepherd.
Yet a Davidic shepherd will shepherd.

Christian readers immediately hear the connection with Jesus.

Jesus says:
“I am the good shepherd.”

He seeks the lost.
He lays down his life for the sheep.

Ezekiel 34 is therefore an important background for John 10.

11. EZEKIEL 36 — A NEW HEART AND A NEW SPIRIT

God promises restoration.

But the deepest problem is not geography.

The people need inner renewal.

God says:

“I will give you a new heart,
and a new spirit I will put within you.”

“I will remove the heart of stone.”

“I will put my Spirit within you.”

This is one of the Old Testament’s clearest promises of inner transformation.

Christian theology connects this promise with:
regeneration,
the Holy Spirit,
and the new covenant.

God does not merely command changed behavior.

He promises to change people from within.

12. EZEKIEL 37 — THE VALLEY OF DRY BONES

Ezekiel sees a valley filled with dry bones.

God asks:
“Can these bones live?”

Humanly, no.

Ezekiel answers wisely:
“O Lord GOD, you know.”

The bones come together.
Breath enters them.
They live.

THE IMMEDIATE MEANING

The vision represents Israel in exile.

The people say:
“Our bones are dried up... our hope is lost.”

God promises national restoration.

CHRISTIAN CONNECTION

The passage is not originally a direct prediction of individual bodily resurrection.

Yet it fits a larger biblical pattern in which God brings life from death.

Christians naturally read it in the light of:
Christ’s resurrection,
spiritual regeneration,
and final resurrection.

THE TWO STICKS

Judah and Israel are pictured reunited.

God promises one shepherd and one people.

13. EZEKIEL 38–39 — GOG AND THE FINAL ENEMY

Gog of Magog appears as a great enemy attacking God’s restored people.

The imagery is dramatic and has generated endless speculation.

Christians should be cautious about identifying Gog with whichever modern nation currently dominates the news.

The point of the passage is more durable:
even after restoration,
hostile evil remains,
but God will finally defeat it.

Revelation 20 later reuses “Gog and Magog” symbolically for the final gathering of rebellious nations.

That reuse should warn us against overly literal modern newspaper decoding.

STUDY QUESTION

Why is interpreting Gog primarily through the Bible’s own later use safer than identifying Gog with a current nation?

14. EZEKIEL 40–48 — THE TEMPLE VISION

Ezekiel sees a vast restored temple.

The measurements are detailed.

God’s glory returns.

The book that earlier watched glory leave now watches glory come back.

Christians interpret the temple vision in several ways.

Some expect a future literal temple.
Some understand the vision symbolically as ideal restored worship.
Some see it fulfilled through Christ and the church.
Many see it reaching ultimately toward new creation.

REVELATION

The strongest clue for Christian canonical reading is how Revelation uses Ezekiel.

Revelation’s final city has:
temple-like dimensions,
a life-giving river,
trees,
and God dwelling with his people.

Yet Revelation says there is no temple building because God and the Lamb are its temple.

The trajectory moves toward direct divine presence.

STUDY QUESTION

15. EZEKIEL 47 — THE RIVER OF LIFE

Water flows from the temple.

At first shallow,
then deeper,
then impossible to cross.

Where the river goes,
dead places live.

Even the Dead Sea becomes fruitful.

Trees grow along the banks.

Their fruit provides food.
Their leaves provide healing.

This is one of Scripture’s great images of God’s life spreading outward.

Revelation 22 uses similar river and tree imagery in the new creation.

God’s presence does not merely occupy sacred space.

It brings life to everything it touches.

16. MAJOR THEMES, KEY PASSAGES, AND REVIEW

GLORY
God’s presence is majestic and cannot be controlled.

IDOLATRY
Religious institutions cannot protect people who reject God.

RESPONSIBILITY
People are accountable for their own response to God.

SHEPHERD
God condemns exploitative leaders and promises a true shepherd.

NEW HEART
Restoration must reach the inner person.

SPIRIT
God’s Spirit creates obedience and life.

RESURRECTION

17. “THEN THEY WILL KNOW THAT I AM THE LORD”

A phrase repeats throughout Ezekiel:
“Then they will know that I am the LORD.”

It appears after:
judgment,
deliverance,
prophetic signs,
and restoration.

The phrase tells us Ezekiel is deeply concerned with God being truly known.

Idolatry distorts knowledge of God.

False confidence distorts knowledge of God.

Judgment exposes false pictures.

Restoration reveals grace.

Christian faith is not merely receiving benefits from God.

The goal is knowing God himself.

18. EZEKIEL AND THE HOLY SPIRIT

Ezekiel gives major Old Testament background for the New Testament’s teaching about spiritual renewal.

Ezekiel 36:
God puts his Spirit within his people.

Ezekiel 37:
breath/Spirit brings dry bones to life.

The Hebrew word ruach can mean:
wind,
breath,
or spirit,
depending on context.

The wordplay is intentional in Ezekiel 37.

Life comes because God breathes life into death.

Jesus tells Nicodemus that people must be born of the Spirit.

Paul says the Spirit who raised Jesus gives life.

Christian regeneration is therefore not merely moral self-improvement.

It is divine life given to spiritually dead people.

19. EZEKIEL AND CHRISTIAN LEADERSHIP

Ezekiel 34 should be required reading for spiritual leaders.

Bad shepherds:
feed themselves,
use the flock,
ignore the weak,
fail to seek the lost.

God’s anger is directed not at leadership itself but at leadership used for self-service.

Jesus reverses that model.

The Good Shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.

Christian leadership therefore moves downward in service rather than upward toward privilege.

Pastors,
teachers,
parents,
managers,
and Christian leaders
should all feel the weight of that principle.

20. DIFFICULT PASSAGES IN EZEKIEL

THE WHEELS

Do not turn Ezekiel 1 into alien spacecraft speculation.

The vision communicates divine glory, movement, and sovereignty.

THE EXTREME SYMBOLIC LANGUAGE

Some passages use graphic sexual and violent imagery.

Read them as prophetic shock language while recognizing how painful such imagery may be to readers with histories of abuse.

THE TEMPLE SACRIFICES

Ezekiel’s future temple includes sacrifices.

Christians who believe Christ’s sacrifice is final interpret these chapters differently.

Some see a future commemorative system.
Others see symbolic idealized worship.
Others see the whole vision transformed in Christ and new creation.

This is a legitimate area of disagreement among Christians.

GOG

Avoid mapping Gog confidently onto contemporary Russia, China, Iran, or another current state.

Every generation is tempted to make its newspaper the key to prophecy.

Scripture itself should control the interpretation.

21. EZEKIEL READING PLAN

READ 1–11
Glory, call, temple corruption, departing presence.

READ 12–24
Signs and judgment against Jerusalem.

READ 25–32
Judgment of surrounding nations.

READ 33–39
Watchman, shepherd, new heart, dry bones, Gog.

READ 40–48
Temple, land, river, returning glory.

TRACK ONE QUESTION THROUGH THE WHOLE BOOK

Where is God’s presence?

At the beginning:
God appears in exile.

Then:
glory leaves the corrupt temple.

At the end:
“The LORD Is There.”

That movement is the heart of Ezekiel.
