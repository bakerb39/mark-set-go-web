ABOUT DANIEL

Daniel teaches believers how to live faithfully when the surrounding culture does not share their deepest loyalties.

The first half contains famous court stories:
Daniel and his friends in Babylon,
the fiery furnace,
Nebuchadnezzar’s humiliation,
the writing on the wall,
Daniel in the lions’ den.

The second half contains apocalyptic visions involving beasts, kingdoms, persecution, judgment, resurrection, and the final victory of God.

Daniel therefore combines:
faithful living in exile
with hope in God’s future kingdom.

Daniel 1–6 — stories in foreign courts
Daniel 7–12 — apocalyptic visions

The book contains Hebrew and Aramaic sections, reflecting its distinctive literary shape.

1. DANIEL 1 — FAITHFUL IN BABYLON

Daniel and his friends are taken from Judah to Babylon.

Their names are changed.
They are educated in Babylonian literature.
They are prepared for imperial service.

Babylon does not merely want their labor.

It wants to reshape their identity.

Yet Daniel chooses a point of faithfulness concerning the royal food.

Notice his approach.

He is not rude.
He is not needlessly provocative.
He works respectfully within the system while preserving obedience to God.

That is a useful model for Christians living in pluralistic societies:
engage,
learn,
serve,
but know where loyalty to God sets limits.

2. DANIEL 2 — THE DREAM OF THE KINGDOMS

Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a statue composed of different materials.

Daniel reveals and interprets the dream.

Successive kingdoms rise and fall.

Then a stone not cut by human hands strikes the statue and becomes a mountain filling the earth.

The central message is clear:
human empires are temporary.
God’s kingdom is not.

Political powers can appear permanent when we live inside them.

Daniel teaches historical humility.

Every empire has an expiration date.

CHRISTIAN CONNECTION

Jesus announces:
“The kingdom of God is at hand.”

Christians see God’s final kingdom centered in Christ and ultimately filling creation.

3. DANIEL 3 — THE FIERY FURNACE

Nebuchadnezzar creates a great image and demands worship.

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refuse.

Their response is one of Scripture’s strongest expressions of faithful courage:

God is able to deliver us.

“But if not...”

Even if God does not rescue them,
they will not worship the idol.

That is mature faith.

Faith is not:
God must give me the outcome I want.

Faith says:
God is able,
God is worthy,
and I will remain faithful regardless of the immediate outcome.

A fourth figure appears with them in the fire.

Christians have often seen a foreshadowing of Christ’s presence, though the text identifies the figure in heavenly terms without giving the later Christian identification explicitly.

4. DANIEL 4 — THE HUMILIATION OF NEBUCHADNEZZAR

Nebuchadnezzar boasts over Babylon.

“Is not this great Babylon, which I have built... by my mighty power?”

His pride is immediately judged.

The great king is reduced to animal-like existence.

Then he lifts his eyes toward heaven and his reason returns.

The lesson is explicit:
Heaven rules.

Human greatness becomes madness when it forgets creaturely dependence.

Pride distorts reality.

Humility restores sanity.

5. DANIEL 5 — THE WRITING ON THE WALL

Belshazzar holds a feast using vessels taken from Jerusalem’s temple.

The act combines arrogance, sacrilege, and false security.

A mysterious hand writes on the wall.

Daniel interprets:
the king has been weighed,
found wanting,
and his kingdom will fall.

That very night Babylon falls.

The phrase “the writing on the wall” entered ordinary speech because the scene captures a universal truth:
judgment can be near while powerful people feel safest.

Belshazzar knew what happened to Nebuchadnezzar.

Yet he did not humble himself.

Knowing history does not guarantee learning from history.

6. DANIEL 6 — THE LIONS’ DEN

Daniel serves under a new empire.

His enemies cannot find corruption in him.

So they weaponize his faithfulness.

A law is designed to make Daniel’s ordinary prayer illegal.

Daniel does not stage a dramatic protest.

He simply continues his normal practice:
he prays.

This is quiet courage.

Daniel’s faithfulness existed before the crisis.

That is why it survives the crisis.

God delivers Daniel from the lions.

But Daniel 3 has already taught us not to make deliverance automatic.

The point is not:
faithful believers never suffer.

The point is:
God remains sovereign, and obedience remains right.

7. WHAT IS APOCALYPTIC LITERATURE?

Daniel 7–12 feels different.

We encounter:
beasts,
horns,
angels,
symbolic numbers,
heavenly conflict,
and visions of the end.

This is apocalyptic literature.

Apocalyptic uses vivid symbols to reveal the spiritual meaning of history.

It is especially written for people living under oppressive power.

The message is not:
decode every symbol into tomorrow’s newspaper.

The message is:
what looks unbeatable from earth looks temporary from heaven.

APOCALYPTIC READING RULE

Begin with what the text meant to suffering believers in its own world.

Then follow how later Scripture reuses the imagery.

Only then consider modern application.

This prevents sensational interpretation.

8. DANIEL 7 — THE BEASTS AND THE SON OF MAN

Four terrifying beasts rise from the sea.

They represent kingdoms.

Human empire becomes beastly.

Then the scene changes.

The Ancient of Days sits in judgment.

The beasts lose power.

Then:
“one like a son of man”
comes with the clouds
and receives everlasting dominion.

This is one of the most important Old Testament backgrounds for Jesus’ title:
“Son of Man.”

Jesus frequently calls himself the Son of Man.

At his trial, he uses Daniel 7 language about coming with the clouds.

The claim is enormous.

Jesus identifies himself with the figure who receives universal kingdom from God.

THE SAINTS

Daniel 7 also connects the kingdom with “the saints of the Most High.”

The Son of Man is both an individual figure and representative of God’s people.

Christians see this fulfilled beautifully in Christ and those united to him.

9. DANIEL 8 — PERSECUTION AND THE DESECRATION OF WORSHIP

Daniel sees a ram, a goat, horns, and a ruler who attacks God’s people and desecrates worship.

The vision is commonly connected with conflicts involving later Greek rule and especially Antiochus IV Epiphanes, who violently oppressed Jewish worship in the second century BC.

The historical details matter because apocalyptic hope is born in real persecution.

God’s people are not promised that evil rulers cannot cause terrible damage.

They are promised that evil rulers have limits.

Their time ends.

STUDY QUESTION

Why is “evil has a limit” different from saying “evil cannot hurt us”?

10. DANIEL 9 — CONFESSION AND THE SEVENTY WEEKS

Daniel studies Jeremiah’s prophecy concerning exile.

His response is prayer.

He confesses:
“We have sinned.”

Daniel personally appears more faithful than most characters around him.

Yet he identifies with his people.

This is mature corporate confession.

He does not say:
They sinned.

He says:
We sinned.

THE SEVENTY WEEKS

Gabriel gives a difficult prophecy involving “seventy weeks.”

Interpretations vary significantly.

Christian approaches include:
schemes focused on events leading to Antiochus,
messianic readings centered on Christ,
and futurist readings involving a final tribulation.

This is an area where humility is appropriate.

The central message is clearer than every chronological detail:
God has a determined purpose for sin, judgment, atonement, and final righteousness.

11. DANIEL 10 — SPIRITUAL CONFLICT

Daniel’s prayer is followed by a strange glimpse of heavenly conflict.

An angelic messenger speaks of resistance from the “prince of Persia” and assistance from Michael.

The passage suggests that earthly events have a spiritual dimension humans normally do not see.

Christians should avoid turning this into a detailed map of territorial demons that the text itself does not provide.

The sober lesson is:
reality is larger than the visible world.

Prayer participates in realities we may not fully perceive.

12. DANIEL 11 — HISTORY UNDER GOD’S RULE

Daniel 11 contains detailed conflicts among rulers and kingdoms.

The chapter can feel overwhelming.

But that may partly be the point.

Kings rise.
Kings scheme.
Wars happen.
Alliances form.
Alliances collapse.

From the ground, history looks chaotic.

From the prophetic viewpoint, none of it escapes God.

The chapter also portrays rulers whose arrogance becomes increasingly extreme.

Human power tends to overreach when it forgets accountability.

13. DANIEL 12 — RESURRECTION AND THE END

Daniel ends with one of the clearest Old Testament statements about resurrection:

“Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake.”

Some to everlasting life.
Some to shame.

This is crucial.

Biblical hope is not only:
our nation survives,
our circumstances improve,
or our enemies fall.

God’s final answer reaches beyond death.

The wise will shine.

Daniel is told to go his way and rest.

He will stand in his allotted place at the end.

The book closes not with a chart but with hope.

14. DANIEL AND CHRIST

Daniel contributes major language to the New Testament.

SON OF MAN
Jesus’ favorite self-designation is deeply connected to Daniel 7.

KINGDOM
Jesus announces God’s kingdom as the final rule that overturns the kingdoms of this world.

ABOMINATION OF DESOLATION
Jesus reuses Daniel’s language when warning about judgment.

RESURRECTION
Daniel 12 contributes to the biblical hope Jesus and the apostles proclaim.

FAITHFUL WITNESS
Daniel and his friends provide patterns of allegiance to God under hostile political systems.

REVELATION
The book of Revelation is saturated with Daniel’s beasts, horns, thrones, kingdoms, and final judgment.

To understand Revelation well, know Daniel first.

15. MAJOR THEMES, KEY PASSAGES, AND REVIEW

GOD RULES HISTORY
Empires rise and fall under God’s ultimate sovereignty.

FAITHFUL EXILE
Believers can serve society without worshiping society.

COURAGE
Obedience is right even when deliverance is uncertain.

PRIDE
Rulers become beastly when they absolutize their own power.

KINGDOM
God’s kingdom outlasts every human kingdom.

SON OF MAN
The truly human ruler receives everlasting authority.

PERSECUTION
Evil may wound the saints but cannot finally defeat God.

RESURRECTION
God’s final victory reaches beyond death.

16. DANIEL AND CHRISTIAN LIFE IN A PLURALISTIC SOCIETY

Daniel does not withdraw from Babylon.

He learns its language.
He receives education.
He serves pagan kings.
He becomes excellent at his work.

Yet there are boundaries he will not cross.

This gives Christians a mature model.

Two opposite mistakes are possible.

ASSIMILATION

Blend in so completely that faith no longer creates meaningful difference.

ISOLATION

Refuse meaningful engagement with neighbors and institutions.

Daniel does neither.

He serves Babylon without worshiping Babylon.

Christians today can:
work in secular institutions,
learn from people who disagree,
serve the common good,
and participate in civic life,
while keeping ultimate loyalty to Christ.

17. DANIEL AND POLITICAL IDOLATRY

Daniel sees political power realistically.

Government can provide order.

Daniel and his friends serve governments.

But government can also demand worship.

Nebuchadnezzar builds an image.
Darius’s law temporarily places the king where prayer belongs.
The beasts of Daniel 7 portray empires becoming monstrous.

The Bible does not require Christians to treat politics as dirty and irrelevant.

But it absolutely forbids treating political power as messianic.

No president,
king,
party,
nation,
or movement
is the kingdom of God.

Political involvement should therefore be serious but never worshipful.

18. HOW TO READ DANIEL’S PROPHECIES WITHOUT FEAR

Prophecy can produce anxiety when readers assume they must decode every symbol correctly.

Daniel’s purpose is the opposite.

The visions are meant to strengthen believers.

The beasts look terrifying.

But the Ancient of Days is already seated.

The saints suffer.

But the kingdom is given to them.

History becomes chaotic.

But God has not lost control.

Death comes.

But resurrection follows.

Therefore, when studying difficult prophecy:

Hold the clear truths tightly.
Hold speculative timelines lightly.

Clear:
God rules.
Evil is temporary.
The saints may suffer.
God judges.
The Son of Man reigns.
Resurrection comes.

Debated:
precise identities,
chronologies,
modern political correspondences.

STUDY QUESTION

How can Christians become more confident in biblical prophecy while becoming less confident in speculative predictions?

19. DANIEL AND PRAYER

Daniel’s public courage grows out of private prayer.

Chapter 6 tells us prayer was his normal habit.

Chapter 9 gives extended confession.

Chapter 10 shows persistent prayer amid delayed answers.

Daniel does not pray only when crisis arrives.

Prayer forms him before crisis.

His prayers include:
worship,
confession,
Scripture,
petition,
and perseverance.

Notice also that Daniel’s prayer in chapter 9 is shaped by reading Jeremiah.

Good Bible reading produces prayer.

Good prayer returns us to God’s revealed character.

How might Daniel’s prayer life explain his unusual steadiness under pressure?

20. DANIEL READING PLAN

DAY 1 — Daniel 1–2
Faithful exile and temporary kingdoms.

DAY 2 — Daniel 3–4
Courage and humbled pride.

DAY 3 — Daniel 5–6
Judgment and faithful prayer.

DAY 4 — Daniel 7
Beasts, Ancient of Days, Son of Man.

DAY 5 — Daniel 8–9
Persecution, confession, prophetic timetable.

DAY 6 — Daniel 10–12
Spiritual conflict, history, resurrection.

AFTER EACH READING ASK

What does human power look like?
What does God’s power look like?
What does faithful obedience look like?
What hope is offered to suffering believers?
