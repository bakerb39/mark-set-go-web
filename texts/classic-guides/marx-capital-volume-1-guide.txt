AT A GLANCE

Capital analyzes capitalism not primarily through moral denunciation but through the internal logic of commodity production, wage labor, profit, competition, and accumulation. Marx asks how a society of formally free exchange can systematically generate surplus and class domination.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Marx begin with the commodity rather than with poverty or political revolution?

THE COMMODITY

A commodity has use value and exchange value. Market exchange abstracts from concrete qualities and makes unlike goods comparable through social relations of production.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does exchange require a common social measure among very different useful things?

COMMODITY FETISHISM

Relations among people in production appear as relations among things and prices. The market can obscure the social labor and institutional structures that produced commodities.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What does it mean for a social relationship to appear as a property of an object?

MONEY

Money becomes the universal equivalent that simplifies exchange and stores value. It also enables the transformation from simple circulation to capital: money advanced in order to return as more money.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What changes when exchange is organized around accumulation rather than use?

LABOR POWER

The key commodity is the worker’s capacity to labor. Workers can be legally free while lacking independent access to the means of production, making wage labor economically necessary.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How can a contract be formally voluntary while occurring within unequal material conditions?

SURPLUS VALUE

Workers can create more value during the working day than the value represented by their wages. Marx locates profit’s underlying source in this surplus labor rather than in unequal exchange alone.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why is exploitation in Marx’s theory compatible with workers being paid the market value of labor power?

THE WORKING DAY

Conflict over hours exposes opposed interests between capital and labor. Marx’s historical chapters show how law, organization, and struggle shape what might otherwise appear to be a purely economic contract.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does the length of the working day become a political question?

RELATIVE SURPLUS VALUE

Productivity gains can reduce the labor time needed to reproduce workers’ consumption, expanding surplus even without extending hours. Cooperation, division of labor, and machinery transform production.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How can rising productivity increase both social wealth and pressure on workers?

MACHINERY

Machinery can lighten labor, but under capitalist organization it can intensify work, deskill tasks, displace workers, and increase managerial control. Technology’s social effect depends on institutional context.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Marx distinguish technology itself from the way technology is organized under capital?

PRIMITIVE ACCUMULATION

Capitalism did not arise simply because some people saved more carefully. Marx traces enclosure, colonialism, dispossession, and state power in the historical creation of wage labor and concentrated property.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Marx challenge peaceful origin stories of capitalist property relations?

ACCUMULATION

Competition pushes firms to reinvest, expand, innovate, and concentrate capital. Accumulation therefore becomes systemic rather than merely the result of personal greed.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does competition compel behavior even from individual capitalists who might prefer otherwise?

LEGACY AND CRITIQUE

Capital shaped economics, sociology, labor history, political theory, and critiques of inequality. Critics dispute Marx’s value theory, predictions, and political conclusions, while many still use his concepts of class, commodification, and accumulation.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which parts of Marx’s analysis remain useful even for readers who reject his revolutionary conclusions?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the work’s central problem in one sentence.
2. Identify its strongest argument, insight, or dramatic achievement.
3. Distinguish description from endorsement.
4. Track the work’s major causal, conceptual, or character relationships.
5. Identify one historical assumption the work depends on.
6. Explain one major term, image, experiment, or conflict in the work’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection or criticism.
9. Identify one insight that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.

DEEPER READING LABS

LAB 1 — ARGUMENT OR PLOT MAP
Map the work’s central movement. For argument, identify the thesis and strongest supports. For literature, identify the decisive choices, reversals, and consequences.

LAB 2 — KEY DISTINCTIONS
Identify five distinctions or oppositions the work relies on and explain what becomes difficult to understand if each is blurred.

LAB 3 — HISTORICAL TEST
Choose one historical assumption, institution, scientific limitation, or social practice and evaluate how much the work depends on it.

LAB 4 — STRONGEST OBJECTION
Construct the strongest objection you can, then answer it using evidence from the work itself.

LAB 5 — GREAT BOOKS CONNECTION
Compare this work with at least two earlier authors on freedom, morality, nature, history, society, science, religion, or human character.
