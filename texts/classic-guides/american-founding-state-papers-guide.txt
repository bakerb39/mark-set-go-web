AT A GLANCE

This guide treats the Declaration of Independence, Articles of Confederation, and Constitution as a connected sequence. The Declaration states a theory of legitimate government; the Articles create a confederation of sovereign states; the Constitution redesigns the Union around stronger federal institutions.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What problems remain constant across all three documents, and what changes most dramatically?

THE DECLARATION — PURPOSE AND AUDIENCE

The Declaration is simultaneously an act of diplomacy, political justification, indictment, and statement of principles. Its argument must persuade domestic audiences, foreign powers, and posterity that separation is legitimate.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does audience shape both the philosophical claims and the grievances?

NATURAL RIGHTS AND EQUALITY

The Declaration’s enduring claims concern equality, unalienable rights, consent, and the right to alter or abolish destructive government. These universal terms coexist with slavery and unequal political participation, creating a central American tension.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How can a political principle exceed the practices of the people who first proclaim it?

THE GRIEVANCES

The grievances convert abstract political theory into a case against patterns of rule: obstruction of legislatures, interference with justice, standing armies, taxation, and suspension of local authority.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does the Declaration need both universal principles and concrete grievances?

THE ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION

The Articles create a firm league among states that retain sovereignty, freedom, and independence. Congress can deliberate and request resources but lacks many direct powers over individuals and revenue.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What advantages did decentralization protect, and what collective-action problems did it create?

STRUCTURAL PROBLEMS

War debts, interstate commerce, revenue, treaty compliance, and security reveal the limits of a system dependent on state compliance. The problem is not merely bad leadership; it is allocation of power.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which weaknesses could have been repaired by amendment, and which required redesign?

WE THE PEOPLE

The Constitution shifts the language of authority from a compact among states toward We the People. It establishes legislative, executive, and judicial institutions with powers that operate directly on individuals.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What conception of sovereignty is implied when federal authority rests on the people?

SEPARATED POWERS

Articles I, II, and III organize legislative, executive, and judicial power. The design is not perfect isolation: vetoes, impeachment, appointments, treaties, funding, and judicial interpretation create interdependence.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does constitutional checking require both separation and overlap?

FEDERALISM

The Constitution grants enumerated powers to the federal government while preserving a continuing role for states. Supremacy, taxation, commerce, and necessary-and-proper powers strengthen national capacity without creating a unitary system.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Where does the Constitution leave ambiguity about the national-state boundary?

REPRESENTATION AND COMPROMISE

The House, Senate, Electoral College, and apportionment rules embody compromises among population, states, democracy, and elite concerns. The original Constitution also protected slavery through structural compromises.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How do compromises that make agreement possible also create long-term moral costs?

RATIFICATION AND AMENDMENT

The Constitution required ratification by conventions and included an amendment mechanism. Constitutional authority must be capable of change without making change easy.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why should a constitution be harder to change than ordinary law but still amendable?

GREAT IDEAS AND LEGACY

Read together, the documents show constitutional development through failure, argument, redesign, and amendment. Their themes are consent, rights, sovereignty, institutional capacity, federalism, and limiting power.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does the Constitution address legitimate power without fully resolving the Declaration’s promise of equality?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the central problem of the work in one sentence.
2. Identify the author’s strongest argument.
3. Distinguish description from prescription.
4. Track the major causal or conceptual relationships.
5. Identify the historical assumptions the argument depends on.
6. Explain one major term in the author’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection to the work.
9. Identify one idea that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.
