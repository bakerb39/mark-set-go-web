ON LIBERTY
An Independent Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide
Original work by John Stuart Mill

A focused guide to individual liberty, social authority, freedom of thought and discussion, individuality, experimentation in living, paternalism, and the harm principle.

ABOUT THIS GUIDE
This independently written educational guide is designed for serious first reading, rereading, and seminar study. It follows the Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide pattern with focused sections, discussion prompts, section quizzes, Great Ideas, review, and whole-guide quiz hooks.

AT A GLANCE

Mill asks where society may legitimately limit individual freedom. His answer is organized around a strong presumption of liberty: coercion is justified to prevent harm to others, not merely because authorities think a person’s choices are foolish or morally wrong.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Mill treat social pressure as a threat to liberty alongside legal coercion?

THE HARM PRINCIPLE

Power may be rightfully exercised over a competent adult against his will only to prevent harm to others. His own good, physical or moral, is not sufficient. The principle is powerful but leaves difficult questions about what counts as harm.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How narrow or broad must harm be if the principle is to protect a meaningful private sphere?

TYRANNY OF THE MAJORITY

Democratic government does not eliminate domination. Majorities can use law, custom, reputation, and moral pressure to suppress dissenting ways of life. Mill shifts the liberty problem from rulers versus subjects to society versus individuals.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why can social conformity be coercive even when no law is violated?

FREEDOM OF DISCUSSION

Suppressing an opinion is dangerous because the opinion may be true, partly true, or false in a way that forces truth to be understood rather than repeated as dead dogma. Debate improves not only discovery but comprehension.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Mill think even false opinions can contribute to knowledge?

FALLIBILITY

No individual or age is infallible. To silence an opposing view is to assume certainty that human beings repeatedly lack. Mill makes epistemic humility a political principle.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What political institutions follow from taking human fallibility seriously?

INDIVIDUALITY

Mill values individuality as a condition of human development. People need room to exercise judgment, choose projects, make mistakes, and form character. Passive conformity weakens the faculties required for mature agency.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Mill connect liberty with development of human capacities?

EXPERIMENTS IN LIVING

Different ways of life function as social experiments. When coercion does not force uniformity, societies can learn from diverse practices. Variation produces social information.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How can a society benefit from lifestyles most citizens do not choose for themselves?

SELF-REGARDING AND OTHER-REGARDING CONDUCT

Mill distinguishes conduct primarily concerning oneself from conduct that harms others. In practice the boundary is difficult because actions have indirect effects.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What criteria can distinguish genuine harm from mere offense, dislike, or moral disapproval?

LIMITS AND EXCEPTIONS

Mill excludes children and those not yet capable of self-government from the full scope of the principle and controversially extends this reasoning to societies he calls barbarous. These passages expose imperial assumptions requiring criticism.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How do Mill’s exclusions complicate the universality of his defense of liberty?

APPLICATIONS

Mill discusses trade, contracts, intoxication, public conduct, education, family authority, and administrative centralization. The examples show that liberty requires drawing lines among prevention, information, regulation, and coercion.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which application best tests the limits of the harm principle?

GREAT IDEAS AND LEGACY

On Liberty became a foundational statement of liberal individualism. Its enduring questions concern free speech, paternalism, social conformity, autonomy, minority rights, and pluralism.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Does the harm principle settle liberty disputes, or mainly clarify what must be argued about?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the central problem of the work in one sentence.
2. Identify the author’s strongest argument.
3. Distinguish description from prescription.
4. Track the major causal or conceptual relationships.
5. Identify the historical assumptions the argument depends on.
6. Explain one major term in the author’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection to the work.
9. Identify one idea that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.
