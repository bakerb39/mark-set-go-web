UTILITARIANISM
An Independent Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide
Original work by John Stuart Mill

A focused guide to happiness, utility, higher and lower pleasures, motivation, justice, rights, sanctions, and the strengths and limits of consequentialist ethics.

ABOUT THIS GUIDE
This independently written educational guide is designed for serious first reading, rereading, and seminar study. It follows the Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide pattern with focused sections, discussion prompts, section quizzes, Great Ideas, review, and whole-guide quiz hooks.

HOW TO USE IT

AT A GLANCE

Mill defends the principle that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness and wrong as they tend to produce the reverse. He also answers objections that utilitarianism is crude, selfish, impractical, or unable to account for justice.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What must Mill add to a simple pleasure calculus to make utilitarianism plausible?

THE GREATEST HAPPINESS PRINCIPLE

Utility identifies happiness—pleasure and absence of pain—as the ultimate end relevant to morality. The theory judges actions by their tendency to affect the well-being of all concerned, not merely the agent.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does utilitarianism require impartial concern rather than egoistic pursuit of one’s own pleasure?

HIGHER AND LOWER PLEASURES

Mill rejects the idea that all pleasures differ only in quantity. Those competent to experience both often prefer intellectual, imaginative, and moral activities even when they bring dissatisfaction.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Can the appeal to competent judges distinguish qualities of pleasure without abandoning utilitarianism?

DIGNITY

The preference for higher pleasures is connected to capacities human beings are unwilling to surrender. Mill makes dignity part of his account of happiness.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Is dignity itself a kind of pleasure, or does it introduce an independent value?

RULES AND CALCULATION

Utilitarianism does not require fresh calculation before every ordinary action. Human beings inherit moral rules developed through long experience about actions that generally promote or damage welfare.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How can consequentialism rely heavily on stable moral rules?

MOTIVATION AND SANCTIONS

Mill distinguishes the criterion of rightness from motives. External sanctions include praise, blame, law, and religion; internal sanction is conscience connected to social feeling.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why is what makes an action right different from what motivates someone to do it?

THE PROOF OF UTILITY

Mill argues that the evidence something is desirable is that people actually desire it, then moves from individual happiness toward general happiness. This proof is heavily criticized because desire and desirability may not be equivalent.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Where does Mill move from psychological fact to moral justification?

JUSTICE

Justice seems resistant to utilitarianism because rights and desert feel categorically binding. Mill argues that justice combines a rule of conduct with a strong sentiment and that the most vital utilities generate especially stringent obligations.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Does explaining justice through utility preserve the force of rights or weaken it?

RIGHTS

To have a right is, for Mill, to have something society ought to defend one’s possession of. Rights are grounded in extremely important utilities, especially security.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does Mill’s account differ from theories that treat rights as prior to utility?

HARD CASES

Utilitarianism faces problems of measurement, demandingness, distribution, sacrifice, and cases in which harming one person might benefit many. Mill offers resources but not final solutions to every modern objection.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What limits should prevent aggregate welfare from overriding individual claims?

GREAT IDEAS AND LEGACY

Mill’s utilitarianism is richer than simple arithmetic. It includes quality of experience, social feeling, rules, conscience, justice, and rights. The lasting challenge is whether all these can ultimately be explained by happiness.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Is Mill refining utilitarianism or importing non-utilitarian values into it?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the central problem of the work in one sentence.
2. Identify the author’s strongest argument.
3. Distinguish description from prescription.
4. Track the major causal or conceptual relationships.
5. Identify the historical assumptions the argument depends on.
6. Explain one major term in the author’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection to the work.
9. Identify one idea that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which one claim from this work most changes the way you understand its subject?
