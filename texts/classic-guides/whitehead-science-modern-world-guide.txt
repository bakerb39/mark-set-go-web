SCIENCE AND THE MODERN WORLD
An Independent Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide
Original work by Alfred North Whitehead

A guide to scientific ideas, nature, abstraction, mechanism, organism, modernity, and Whitehead’s critique of scientific materialism.

ABOUT THIS GUIDE
This independently written educational guide is designed for serious first reading, rereading, and seminar study. It follows the Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide pattern with focused sections, discussion prompts, section quizzes, Great Ideas, review, and whole-guide quiz hooks.

HOW TO USE IT

AT A GLANCE

Whitehead traces how modern science reshaped Western thought while warning that abstractions useful for scientific analysis can become mistaken for the whole of reality. His target is not science but an overly narrow metaphysics built from scientific success.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Whitehead criticize scientific materialism without rejecting science?

THE RISE OF MODERN SCIENCE

The seventeenth century joined mathematical description, experiment, and a mechanistic picture of nature. This transformation produced extraordinary predictive power.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why did mathematical abstraction become so successful in modern science?

THE BIFURCATION OF NATURE

Whitehead criticizes dividing nature into objective physical properties and merely subjective sensory qualities. The world we experience and the world described by physics cannot simply be torn apart.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What problems arise when color, sound, and value are treated as unreal additions to a purely quantitative world?

MISPLACED CONCRETENESS

The fallacy of misplaced concreteness occurs when an abstraction is mistaken for the concrete reality from which it was abstracted. Scientific models are powerful precisely because they leave things out.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why is forgetting what a model omits philosophically dangerous?

MECHANISM

Mechanistic explanation treats nature as matter in motion governed by laws. Whitehead argues that modern physics itself weakened the older picture of inert, self-contained matter.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How did developments in physics make classical materialism less secure?

ORGANISM

Whitehead proposes an organic rather than purely mechanical conception of reality. Events and relations become more fundamental than isolated substances.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What changes when relations are treated as constitutive of things rather than merely external connections?

SPACE, TIME, AND RELATIVITY

Relativity helped undermine absolute space and time, reinforcing Whitehead’s view that nature must be understood through events and relations.

SCIENCE AND VALUE

A worldview that treats nature as valueless matter can leave ethics, beauty, and purpose disconnected from knowledge. Whitehead seeks a broader philosophy that can integrate them.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Can scientific explanation remain rigorous without claiming that value is unreal?

ROMANTIC REACTION

Romantic poets reacted against a disenchanted mechanical nature by emphasizing organism, feeling, beauty, and lived experience. Whitehead treats this reaction as philosophically significant.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What can literature reveal about the limitations of a scientific worldview?

RELIGION

Whitehead sees religion and science as potentially complementary when each avoids dogmatism. Conflict often arises when either side freezes historical formulations into final truth.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What would intellectual humility require from both science and religion?

ABSTRACTION AND EDUCATION

Modern expertise depends on abstraction, yet specialization can fragment knowledge. Whitehead argues for recovering connections among scientific, historical, aesthetic, and philosophical understanding.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why can greater technical knowledge coexist with a narrower worldview?

LEGACY

Science and the Modern World influenced process philosophy, philosophy of science, ecology, theology, and systems thinking. Its enduring warning is that successful abstractions should not be confused with exhaustive descriptions of reality.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which modern technologies most tempt us to mistake models for the reality they model?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the work’s central problem in one sentence.
2. Identify its strongest argument, insight, or achievement.
3. Distinguish description from endorsement.
4. Track the work’s major causal, conceptual, scientific, or interpretive relationships.
5. Identify one historical assumption or limitation the work depends on.
6. Explain one major term, experiment, image, or distinction in the work’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection or criticism.
9. Identify one insight that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.

DEEPER READING LABS

LAB 1 — ARGUMENT MAP
Map the central thesis and the strongest supporting moves. Identify where the author moves from observation to explanation, or from explanation to broader philosophical conclusion.

LAB 2 — KEY DISTINCTIONS
Identify five distinctions the work relies on and explain what becomes difficult to understand if each one is blurred.

LAB 3 — HISTORICAL TEST
Choose one historical assumption, scientific limitation, institution, or cultural context and evaluate how much the work depends on it.

LAB 4 — STRONGEST OBJECTION
Construct the strongest objection you can, then answer it using evidence from the work itself.

LAB 5 — GREAT BOOKS CONNECTION
