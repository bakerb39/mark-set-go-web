LITTLE DORRIT
An Independent Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide
Original work by Charles Dickens

A guide to debt, bureaucracy, imprisonment, class performance, money, family obligation, and social institutions.

ABOUT THIS GUIDE
This independently written educational guide is designed for serious first reading, rereading, and seminar study. It follows the Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide pattern with focused sections, discussion prompts, section quizzes, Great Ideas, review, and whole-guide quiz hooks.

HOW TO USE IT
Read one substantial section at a time. After each section, use Discuss with reading companion for interpretation and Quiz me for a short comprehension check based on that section. Use the final whole-guide quiz after completing the guide.

AT A GLANCE

Little Dorrit connects literal imprisonment in the Marshalsea debtors’ prison with less visible forms of social, bureaucratic, financial, and psychological confinement. Dickens asks how institutions shape character and how people internalize the very systems that restrict them.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does imprisonment become the novel’s organizing metaphor even outside the prison walls?

THE MARSHALSEA

Amy Dorrit grows up inside the debtors’ prison because of her father’s financial collapse. The prison becomes both a material institution and a social world with its own identities and habits.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How can a place of confinement become psychologically familiar enough to feel like home?

AMY DORRIT

Amy’s humility, practical competence, and loyalty contrast with the vanity and self-dramatization around her. Yet her self-effacement also raises questions about how much responsibility one person should carry for a family.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
When does devotion become self-erasure?

WILLIAM DORRIT

Mr. Dorrit adapts to prison by constructing a ceremonial identity as the Father of the Marshalsea. After wealth is restored, he adopts aristocratic pretensions with equal intensity.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What does Dorrit’s changing performance reveal about class identity and shame?

ARTHUR CLENNAM

Arthur returns to England burdened by family guilt and uncertainty. His effort to understand old secrets becomes a moral search for responsibility beyond what the law formally demands.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why is Arthur concerned with obligations he cannot clearly prove?

THE CIRCUMLOCUTION OFFICE

Dickens satirizes bureaucracy through an institution dedicated to discovering 'How not to do it.' Administrative language and procedure turn failure into a self-protecting system.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does bureaucracy preserve itself by making responsibility difficult to locate?

MRS. CLENNAM

The Clennam household embodies moral rigidity, secrecy, and punitive religion. Mrs. Clennam’s certainty about judgment conceals a history of control and concealment.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How can moral certainty become a mechanism for avoiding self-examination?

MONEY AND SPECULATION

The Merdle financial system reveals a society eager to convert reputation into trust and trust into speculation. Apparent respectability becomes a substitute for understanding actual value.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why are respectable institutions vulnerable to fraud when reputation replaces scrutiny?

SOCIAL PERFORMANCE

The novel’s characters constantly perform class position, gentility, success, suffering, or moral authority. Dickens shows status as something theatrically maintained through language and ritual.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What does the novel suggest about the difference between social identity and moral character?

DEBT AS MORAL AND SOCIAL RELATION

Financial debt intersects with gratitude, obligation, guilt, secrecy, and dependence. Dickens resists the idea that every debt can be reduced to a balance sheet.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What kinds of debt cannot be settled simply by paying money?

LIBERATION

Freedom in the novel requires more than opening prison gates. Characters must also escape shame, pride, false identities, institutional dependency, and inherited secrecy.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why can external release fail to produce inner freedom?

LEGACY

Little Dorrit is among Dickens’s darkest institutional novels. Its bureaucracy, finance, debt, and class satire remain recognizable because they show systems that continue functioning precisely when no individual accepts responsibility.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What modern institutions most resemble Dickens’s Circumlocution Office?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the central problem of the work in one sentence.
2. Identify the author’s strongest argument, insight, or dramatic achievement.
3. Distinguish what the work describes from what it endorses.
4. Track the work’s major conceptual, causal, or character relationships.
5. Identify the historical assumptions on which the work depends.
6. Explain one major term, image, or conflict in the work’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection or criticism that can be made.
9. Identify one idea or achievement that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which one claim from this work most changes the way you understand its subject?

DEEPER READING LABS

LAB 1 — ARGUMENT OR PLOT MAP
Trace the central movement of the work. For philosophy and political thought, map the thesis and its strongest supports. For literature, map the decisive choices, reversals, and consequences.

LAB 2 — KEY DISTINCTIONS
Identify five distinctions, contrasts, or oppositions the work relies on. Explain what becomes harder to understand if each one is blurred.

LAB 3 — HISTORICAL TEST
Choose one historical assumption, institution, or social practice in the work and examine how much the work depends on it.

LAB 4 — STRONGEST OBJECTION
Construct the strongest objection you can to the work’s central vision, then answer it using evidence from the work itself.

LAB 5 — GREAT BOOKS CONNECTION
Compare this work with at least two earlier authors on freedom, morality, authority, knowledge, society, religion, nature, or human character.
