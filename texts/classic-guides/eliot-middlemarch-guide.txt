MIDDLEMARCH
An Independent Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide
Original work by George Eliot

A guide to vocation, marriage, reform, sympathy, provincial society, knowledge, money, and the consequences of ordinary choices.

ABOUT THIS GUIDE
This independently written educational guide is designed for serious first reading, rereading, and seminar study. It follows the Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide pattern with focused sections, discussion prompts, section quizzes, Great Ideas, review, and whole-guide quiz hooks.

HOW TO USE IT
Read one substantial section at a time. After each section, use Discuss with reading companion for interpretation and Quiz me for a short comprehension check based on that section. Use the final whole-guide quiz after completing the guide.

AT A GLANCE

Middlemarch interweaves multiple lives to show how ideals are tested by institutions, marriages, money, professional ambitions, and imperfect knowledge. Its great subject is the moral significance of ordinary social life.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Eliot distribute the novel’s meaning across many interconnected characters rather than one heroic plot?

DOROTHEA BROOKE

Dorothea longs for a life of intellectual and moral significance. Her mistake is not having ideals but attaching them to Casaubon, whom she imagines as a great scholar and spiritual guide.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How can admirable aspiration become dangerous when joined to inaccurate judgment?

CASAUBON

Casaubon’s Key to All Mythologies embodies sterile scholarship driven by fear and defensiveness. His tragedy is not simple stupidity; it is the inability to tolerate intellectual vulnerability and emotional reciprocity.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Eliot treat insecurity as central to Casaubon’s failure?

LYDGATE

Tertius Lydgate arrives with genuine scientific ambition and hopes to reform medical practice. His career is gradually compromised by debt, marriage, reputation, and political pressures.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What does Lydgate’s story suggest about the social conditions required for professional integrity?

ROSAMOND VINCy

Rosamond is intelligent in a narrow social sense and highly skilled at shaping appearances. She wants refinement, comfort, and status but resists realities that conflict with her expectations.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does Rosamond exercise power while appearing socially conventional and passive?

FRED VINCy AND MARY GARTH

Fred’s development contrasts with Lydgate’s decline. He learns to abandon fantasies of easy advancement and accepts sustained work. Mary’s moral clarity helps anchor this change.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does the novel treat ordinary responsible work as a meaningful achievement?

BULSTRODE

Bulstrode’s religious respectability conceals a compromised past. His story explores self-deception: people can reinterpret their motives until worldly advantage appears providential.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does self-deception differ from consciously telling a lie?

REFORM AND POLITICS

The Reform Bill, medical reform, newspapers, elections, and local institutions form the historical background. Eliot shows that political change passes through personalities, interests, and incomplete knowledge.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does reform become more complicated when seen at the level of actual communities?

MARRIAGE AS KNOWLEDGE

Each major marriage tests whether people can see another person as a separate reality rather than as a projection of their own desires. Failed marriages are often failures of interpretation before they are failures of affection.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does marriage become an epistemological problem in Middlemarch?

SYMPATHY

Eliot repeatedly enlarges the reader’s perspective beyond any single character’s judgment. Sympathy is not sentimental approval; it is disciplined attention to another person’s circumstances and interior life.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does understanding someone more fully not necessarily mean excusing them?

THE WEB

The novel’s recurring web imagery suggests social interdependence. No action remains purely private because every life is connected to others through family, profession, money, rumor, and memory.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does the web metaphor challenge the fantasy of completely independent action?

THE FINALE

The ending values unhistoric acts and the diffuse effects of ordinary goodness. Dorothea does not become a famous reformer, but Eliot refuses to equate historical fame with moral significance.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What kind of greatness does the novel finally defend?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the central problem of the work in one sentence.
2. Identify the author’s strongest argument, insight, or dramatic achievement.
3. Distinguish what the work describes from what it endorses.
4. Track the work’s major conceptual, causal, or character relationships.
5. Identify the historical assumptions on which the work depends.
6. Explain one major term, image, or conflict in the work’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection or criticism that can be made.
9. Identify one idea or achievement that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Which one claim from this work most changes the way you understand its subject?

DEEPER READING LABS

LAB 1 — ARGUMENT OR PLOT MAP
Trace the central movement of the work. For philosophy and political thought, map the thesis and its strongest supports. For literature, map the decisive choices, reversals, and consequences.

LAB 2 — KEY DISTINCTIONS
Identify five distinctions, contrasts, or oppositions the work relies on. Explain what becomes harder to understand if each one is blurred.

LAB 3 — HISTORICAL TEST
Choose one historical assumption, institution, or social practice in the work and examine how much the work depends on it.

LAB 4 — STRONGEST OBJECTION
Construct the strongest objection you can to the work’s central vision, then answer it using evidence from the work itself.

LAB 5 — GREAT BOOKS CONNECTION
Compare this work with at least two earlier authors on freedom, morality, authority, knowledge, society, religion, nature, or human character.
