THE ODYSSEY
An Independent Mark, Set, Go! Classic Guide
Original work by Homer

A guide to homecoming, identity, hospitality, memory, cunning, justice, and the moral world of Homer’s second great epic.

AT A GLANCE

The Odyssey follows Odysseus as he struggles to return to Ithaca after the Trojan War while his household is consumed by suitors. The poem is about travel, but even more about identity, recognition, hospitality, memory, and the difficult restoration of order after war.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why is returning home morally harder for Odysseus than simply surviving the journey?

TELEMACHUS

The epic begins not with Odysseus but with his son. Telemachus must grow from frustrated dependence into someone capable of judgment, travel, speech, and action.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does the poem make Telemachus’s maturation part of Odysseus’s homecoming story?

XENIA — HOSPITALITY

Hospitality structures the poem’s moral geography. Good hosts feed and protect strangers before demanding information; bad hosts exploit, trap, or consume them.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does hospitality function as a test of civilization throughout the epic?

ODYSSEUS AND CUNNING

Odysseus survives through intelligence, disguise, timing, narrative skill, and self-control. His greatest strengths are also morally ambiguous because cunning can become deception.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
When does cleverness become wisdom, and when does it become manipulation?

THE CYCLOPS

Polyphemus represents a world without law, assembly, agriculture, or hospitality. Odysseus defeats him through intelligence, but his boast afterward reveals the danger of pride.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does Odysseus’s need for recognition nearly undo his successful escape?

CIRCE AND CALYPSO

Both divine women detain Odysseus outside ordinary mortal time. Their islands tempt him with pleasure, safety, and in Calypso’s case immortality, yet he still longs for home.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What does Odysseus’s desire for Ithaca reveal about the value of mortal limits?

THE UNDERWORLD

Odysseus encounters dead heroes, family members, and prophets. The episode reframes glory by confronting the cost of war and the finality of death.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does Achilles’ testimony in the underworld complicate the heroic values of the Iliad?

THE SIRENS, SCYLLA, AND CHARYBDIS

Odysseus must distinguish dangers that can be mastered from those that can only be endured. Leadership sometimes means choosing among losses rather than achieving perfect outcomes.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
What makes accepting unavoidable loss a form of practical wisdom?

PENELOPE

Penelope’s intelligence parallels Odysseus’s. Her weaving, delay, testing, and guarded speech show that fidelity in the poem is active and strategic rather than passive.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
How does Penelope’s cunning complicate simple gender contrasts between home and adventure?

THE SUITORS

The suitors violate hospitality, consume another man’s household, pressure Penelope, and mock social obligations. Their destruction restores order but also raises questions about the scale of vengeance.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why does the poem present the suitors’ behavior as more than ordinary romantic competition?

RECOGNITION

The epic repeatedly stages delayed recognition through scars, stories, tests, and tokens. Identity must be proved, not merely declared.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why is recognition so often connected with memory and suffering?

ENDING AND PEACE

The poem does not end with the slaughter of the suitors. Their relatives seek revenge, and divine intervention is required to stop the cycle.

QUESTION TO CARRY FORWARD
Why must the epic move from vengeance to reconciliation before homecoming is complete?

REVIEW — TEN CLAIMS TO REMEMBER

1. State the work’s central problem in one sentence.
2. Identify its strongest argument, insight, narrative achievement, or explanatory move.
3. Distinguish description from endorsement.
4. Track the work’s major causal, conceptual, historical, or character relationships.
5. Identify one historical assumption or limitation.
6. Explain one major term, image, experiment, or distinction in the work’s own framework.
7. Connect the work to at least two earlier Great Books.
8. State the strongest objection or criticism.
9. Identify one insight that survives even if some conclusions fail.
10. Explain why the work still belongs in a Great Books curriculum.
