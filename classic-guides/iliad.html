The Iliad

Homer · Epic Poetry

Achilles’ rage, heroic honor, mortality, grief, war, and the movement from vengeance toward pity.

1. Before You Read

The Iliad covers only a short period near the end of the Trojan War. Its real subject is the rage of Achilles and the human consequences that follow when the greatest Greek warrior withdraws from battle.

2. Achilles and Agamemnon

Agamemnon humiliates Achilles by taking Briseis, and Achilles treats the insult as an attack on his public worth. The Greek army pays the price for a quarrel between two powerful men.

3. Hector and Troy

Hector broadens the poem beyond Greek glory. He is a warrior, husband, father, and defender of a doomed city. Through him Homer forces the reader to see the enemy as fully human.

4. Patroclus

Patroclus enters battle in Achilles’ armor and is killed. His death transforms Achilles’ anger into grief and vengeance, drawing Achilles back into the war.

5. The Gods and Fate

The gods intervene constantly, but human choices still matter. Homer presents a world in which divine influence, fate, and responsibility overlap.

6. Priam and Achilles

Priam’s appeal for Hector’s body brings Achilles into direct contact with another father’s grief. The poem’s emotional climax is shared mourning rather than conquest.

7. Why It Matters

The Iliad refuses to reduce war to glory. It explores excellence, pride, leadership, love, death, rage, and the fragile possibility of recognizing humanity in an enemy.
