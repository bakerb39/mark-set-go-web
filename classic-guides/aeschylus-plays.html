Aeschylus · Drama Collection

An orientation to Aeschylus: tragic justice, inherited guilt, divine order, civic law, and the movement from blood vengeance toward institutions.

1. Why Read Aeschylus

Aeschylus is one of the earliest surviving Greek tragedians. His drama often places individuals inside enormous moral structures involving family curses, divine claims, political order, and inherited guilt.

2. The Oresteia

Agamemnon, The Libation Bearers, and The Eumenides form the only surviving complete trilogy from Greek tragedy. Together they trace a movement from revenge to legal judgment.

3. Justice Changes Meaning

Across the trilogy, justice begins as reciprocal blood vengeance and ends as civic trial. The plays ask how societies can stop endless cycles of retaliation.

4. Gods and Human Action

Divine forces matter, but human beings still choose, deliberate, deceive, and act. Tragedy arises partly because several moral claims can be valid at once.

5. Family and Violence

Aeschylus repeatedly explores how one generation inherits the moral consequences of another generation’s acts.

6. Public and Private Order

Household crimes become political problems. The health of the city depends on finding forms of justice that can replace vendetta.

7. The Chorus

The chorus interprets events, remembers history, expresses fear, and often embodies the community trying to understand catastrophe.

8. Why It Matters

Aeschylus makes tragedy a laboratory for thinking about responsibility, law, religion, political order, and whether civilization can transform violence rather than merely repeat it.
