FRANKENSTEIN
OR, THE MODERN PROMETHEUS

Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley

Chapter 1

Victor Frankenstein begins the history of his family in Geneva. He remembers his father’s public service, his parents’ devoted marriage, and the warm household in which he was raised. Elizabeth Lavenza becomes part of the family and shares Victor’s childhood with Henry Clerval. The chapter establishes the affection, beauty, and security that surround Victor before his ambitions begin to pull him away from ordinary happiness.

The calm landscape around Lake Geneva, the refinement of the Frankenstein home, and Victor’s early habit of intense reading form the background of his youth. Even in these happy years, his imagination is unusually ardent. He does not merely wish to learn what others know; he longs to discover the hidden causes of the world.

Chapter 2

Victor describes the curiosity that separates him from his companions. He becomes fascinated by the old writers on alchemy and natural philosophy, believing that they may reveal powers overlooked by ordinary science. His father dismisses one of these books without explaining its errors, and Victor’s interest only deepens.

During a violent storm near Geneva, lightning strikes an old tree and reduces it to splinters. A visitor explains the power of electricity and demonstrates newer scientific ideas. The shattered tree becomes a turning point: Victor sees that nature contains forces more astonishing than the dreams of the alchemists, and his desire to master those forces takes a new direction.

Chapter 3

After sorrow enters the Frankenstein household, Victor leaves Geneva to study at the University of Ingolstadt. The departure separates him from Elizabeth, Henry, and the familiar world of his childhood. Grief and ambition travel with him.

At the university, Victor meets professors who represent different attitudes toward science. One mocks his earlier reading, while another speaks with generosity and enthusiasm about modern natural philosophy. Victor is captivated by the promise that scientific inquiry can penetrate nature’s deepest secrets. He resolves to devote himself completely to this pursuit.

Chapter 4

Victor advances rapidly in chemistry, anatomy, and physiology. His studies narrow into a single consuming question: what is the principle of life? He studies decay as well as growth, visits places associated with death, and convinces himself that he has discovered how life may be bestowed upon lifeless matter.

He begins constructing a being of gigantic stature because the smaller structures of the human body would be too difficult to reproduce. The work isolates him. Seasons pass unnoticed, letters go unanswered, and his health declines. What began as intellectual wonder becomes secret obsession, yet Victor continues toward the moment he believes will crown his labor.

Chapter 5

On a dreary night in November, Victor sees the result of his work awaken. The creature opens an eye and begins to breathe. Instead of feeling triumph, Victor is overwhelmed by horror. The beautiful features he had selected combine into something terrifying and unnatural in his sight.

Victor flees the laboratory and wanders in anguish. When he later returns with Henry Clerval, the creature has disappeared. Relief mixes with exhaustion and fear. Victor falls seriously ill, while Henry nurses him without knowing the cause. The act Victor imagined would bring glory has instead begun a chain of suffering he cannot yet comprehend.
